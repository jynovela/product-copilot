# Problem space versus solution space

## What it is

**Solution space** holds anything a customer could use or react to: a live
product, a mockup, a wireframe, a prototype. Once something is built, you
have chosen how it looks, what it does and how it works, whether you meant
to or not (Lean Product Playbook ch2).

**Problem space** holds no product or design. It holds the needs you want
the product to meet: a pain point, a desire, a job to be done or a user
story (ch2).

Strong teams call these the **what** and the **how**. The what is the
benefit the product gives the customer; the how is the design and
technology that deliver it (ch2). In the
[[product-market-fit-pyramid]], target customer, underserved needs and value
proposition sit in problem space; feature set and UX sit in solution space.
The key interface is between the value proposition and the feature set
(ch2).

## Why it matters

- **Wider solutions.** Defining a need with a solution baked in ("a pen that
  works in zero gravity") rules out cheaper answers. A solution-free
  statement ("a way to record notes in zero gravity for later reference")
  lets more ideas in (ch2).
- **Truer view of the market.** A market is a set of related needs, not a
  set of products. Your biggest competitor may be a manual method, like pen
  and paper for tax software (ch2). That is why markets get disrupted: a new
  kind of solution meets the same needs better or much more cheaply (ch2).
- **Less waste.** Inside-out teams build what employees think is good and
  never check it against real needs. Outside-in teams learn needs and what
  customers like and dislike about current solutions before they design
  (ch2).

## When to use

- Writing requirements, a problem statement, or a brief for design.
- Defining who you compete with.
- A team is debating features and nobody can say which need each one meets.
- Someone argues customers can't tell you anything useful.

## Steps

1. **Write the need without a solution.** Strip out any noun that names a
   product type. Test the wording by asking what else could meet it (ch2).
2. **Use far-out ideas to refine it.** Even an unworkable idea shows what the
   need leaves out. In the pen example, a voice recorder shows that "no
   power source" and "easy to scan later" belong in the need (ch2).
3. **Name the market by the need.** List every way people meet it today,
   including doing nothing, doing it by hand, or paying someone (ch2).
4. **Learn needs from current solutions.** Interview and observe people
   using existing products (contextual inquiry or customer discovery). Note
   pain points they hit even when they don't mention them, and ask what they
   like and dislike (ch2).
5. **Then show them solution artifacts.** Customers struggle to discuss
   abstract benefits and rank them, and are often wrong when they try. They
   are much better at reacting to a design: what they like, what they don't,
   and how it compares (ch2). Feed what you learn back into your
   problem-space hypotheses.
6. **Alternate.** Move between the two spaces on purpose as you test and
   improve your hypotheses (ch2, ch10).

## Should you listen to customers?

Critics say customers won't invent breakthroughs ("a faster horse"). That's
true, but they aren't designers, so nobody should expect it. The mistake is
using that as an excuse not to learn their needs (ch2). Understanding needs
lets the team explore new solutions and estimate how much each would be
worth (ch2). A team can be confident in an untested feature when it clearly
beats the old way on benefits customers already care about. It still risks
side effects that a few user tests would catch (ch2). Customer feedback is
like a flashlight: it won't lead you to the breakthrough, but it keeps you
from walking off a cliff (ch2).

## Good and bad signs

- Good: requirements state the benefit and leave the design open.
- Good: the competitor list includes non-product alternatives.
- Bad: a "need" names the feature ("users need a dashboard").
- Bad: a change ships with no customer benefit anyone can name. It is
  probably serving internal goals like looks or cost (ch2).

## Pitfalls

- Jumping to solutions too early, so you fix on one expensive approach (ch2).
- Asking customers to rank abstract needs and trusting the answer (ch2).
- Treating "customers don't know what they want" as permission to skip them
  (ch2).
- Using internal testing alone. Dogfooding helps, but employees aren't
  always a good proxy (ch2).
