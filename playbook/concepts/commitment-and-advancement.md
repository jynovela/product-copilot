# Commitment and advancement

## What it is

Once you show your idea, compliments come back. Cut through them by
asking for something of value (Mom Test ch5).

- **Commitment:** they give up something they value: time, reputation or
  money. The more they give, the more you can trust what they say.
- **Advancement:** they move to the next step of your real buying process.

Every meeting succeeds (a commitment to a next step) or fails (a compliment
or a stall). There is no meeting that "went well". Rejection isn't failure;
not asking is (ch5).

## When to use

- After you've learned the facts and have something to show: wireframes, a
  prototype, a product.
- Reviewing a pipeline to tell real leads from polite ones.

## Steps

1. Before the meeting, decide what commitment and next step you'll ask for
   (ch8).
2. Start with open learning questions anyway ([[mom-test-interviews]]).
3. Show the thing, then ask for a commitment they can make today.
4. Turn vague offers into specifics: who, when, what "ready" means, why
   not now.
5. If the trial is too cheap to mean anything, raise its cost: team use for
   a week, a case study after two weeks, a card on file with free
   cancellation within 30 days (ch5).
6. Know the next steps yourself. Answering "What are the next steps?" with
   "Let me get back to you" wastes a good meeting.

## Currencies

| Currency | Examples (ch5) |
| --- | --- |
| Time | next meeting with a set goal; feedback session on wireframes; real use of a trial |
| Reputation | intro to peers or team; intro to a decision maker (boss, spouse, lawyer); testimonial or case study |
| Money | letter of intent; pre-order; deposit |

The strongest combine them, such as a paid trial with the whole team.

## Reading the ending

| They say (ch5) | Verdict | Next move |
| --- | --- | --- |
| "That's so cool. I love it!" | Bad | Deflect, get back to facts |
| "Let me know when it launches." | Bad, a polite no | Ask for something today: intro to the budget holder, alpha with part of the team |
| "I can intro you to people when you're ready." | Weak | Ask who, and what "ready" means |
| "What are the next steps?" | Good | Only if you know them |
| "I would definitely buy that." | Bad, high false-positive risk | Ask for a letter of intent, pre-order or intros |
| "When can we start the trial?" | Maybe | Depends on what the trial costs them |
| "Can I buy the prototype?" | Great | Sell it |
| "When can you come back to meet the team?" | Good | In B2B, no intros means a dead end |

## Early customers

First customers want it badly enough to try it before it's rational. In
enterprise they have the problem, know they have it, have budget, and have
already built a workaround; they pay from discretionary budget or fight
their own lawyers for you. In consumer they pre-order a rough prototype and
already search for workarounds. Someone who isn't emotional about the
problem is unlikely to buy first; keep the emotional ones close (ch5).

## Good and bad signs

- Bad: zombie leads who keep meeting and never buy; meetings ending on a
  compliment or with no next step; "it went well"; they gave nothing up.
- Good: a next step with a date and a goal; something given up today.

## Pitfalls

- Being pushy. You aren't strong-arming; you're forcing a decision so you
  learn where you stand (ch5).
- Even next steps can be a lie; weight signals by what they cost.
- Consumer signals are murkier than enterprise ones, because the
  conversation doesn't mirror how consumers buy (ch5).
