# Vanity metrics

## What it is

A metric that looks impressive but doesn't inform a decision or tie to a goal: page views, likes, total signups, downloads (Semrush, Asana, Giannakopoulos, Gupta, Product School). Xray extends the idea to team-comparison productivity metrics such as velocity per team; Holmes calls raw API calls borderline vanity. (see [[2026-11-24-research-digest-metrics]])

## How to use it

- Ask "what decision will this metric help us make?" (Product School). (see [[2026-11-24-research-digest-metrics]])
- Ask "what value of this metric will prove that we were wrong?" (Giannakopoulos). (see [[2026-11-24-research-digest-metrics]])
- Prefer outcomes to activity: task completion over clicks (Product School). (see [[2026-11-24-research-digest-metrics]])
- A good metric is measurable, impactful, actionable, readable and relevant (Giannakopoulos). (see [[2026-11-24-research-digest-metrics]])

## Watch out for

- Partial metrics cover a small part of the story, so a fall may mean nothing (Giannakopoulos). (see [[2026-11-24-research-digest-metrics]])
- Metrics built to validate a preset view tell the wrong story (Giannakopoulos). (see [[2026-11-24-research-digest-metrics]])

## What Escaping the Build Trap adds

Source note: from secondary summaries of the book, not the book text. Chapter numbers are the book's.

- Totals such as users, page views or logins look good because they only
  grow. Add a time frame to make them actionable: more users than last
  month, and what did we do differently? (Build Trap ch16).
- Output metrics, such as features shipped or story points, are a second
  trap: they measure productivity, not the product (ch16).
