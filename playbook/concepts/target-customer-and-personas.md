# Target customer and personas

## What it is

The target customer is the bottom layer of the
[[product-market-fit-pyramid]]. Needs belong to a segment: different
customers have different needs, and even people with the same needs rank
them differently (Lean Product Playbook ch3). You start with a hypothesis,
but you only learn who your customers are once you put a product or
prototype out and see who bites. It may attract more than one type (ch3).

A persona is a one-page, hypothetical archetype of a real user and what
they want to achieve (ch3). Olsen uses personas early, to record target
customer hypotheses, not only in design (ch3).

## When to use

- Starting a new product, or moving up market, down market or into an
  adjacent vertical (ch3).
- The team disagrees about who the product is for.
- Many people make small product decisions on their own and need a shared
  picture of the customer (ch3).

## Steps

### 1. Segment the market

Describe the target customer with every attribute that matters (ch3):

- **Demographic:** age, gender, income, education. For businesses, use
  firmographics such as company size and industry.
- **Psychographic:** attitudes, values, interests. Often more useful than
  demographics, which are frequently incidental (ch3).
- **Behavioral:** whether and how often people take an action, such as
  "places 10 or more trades a week" (ch3).
- **Needs-based:** groups with distinct needs. This can unite groups no
  single demographic fits, such as parents, pet owners and shops all
  needing to watch video remotely. Each group still has its own detailed
  needs, so tailor the marketing and features to each (ch3).

### 2. Separate users from buyers

In business products, the user is often not the buyer. Name the
**economic buyer**, who controls the budget, and the other stakeholders
who can block the deal over unmet requirements. Buyers have their own
needs; define a target buyer too when it applies (ch3).

### 3. Place the market on the adoption lifecycle

Innovators, early adopters, early majority, late majority and laggards
differ in how much risk they accept (ch3). Early on you may target
innovators, who will pay more and forgive flaws outside the core
innovation. Later segments want more ease of use, reliability and a lower
price, so the product has to change before they adopt (ch3). See
[[technology-adoption-lifecycle]].

### 4. Write a persona

Fit it on one page (ch3):

- Name, a representative photo, and a quote that says what they care about
  most
- Job title and demographics
- Needs and goals; motivations and attitudes
- Related tasks and behaviors
- Frustrations with current solutions
- Domain expertise
- Usage context, such as a laptop in a loud office or a tablet on the couch
- Adoption lifecycle segment for your category

The name, photo and quote are what people remember (ch3).

### 5. Ground it in data and iterate

- With customers: interviews first, then surveys once you know what to ask
  (ch3).
- Without customers: draft from judgment, then test it in customer
  discovery interviews with people who fit the profile (ch3).
- Never fill a persona with survey averages. Nobody has 2.3 children. Use
  the aggregate data to check the persona covers a real share of
  customers, and read individual responses from people who fit (ch3).
- Revise it as you learn. When a product attracts a distinct second group,
  write a second persona (ch3).
- Talk to your most passionate customers, the ones who use it most and
  recommend it. They show which underserved needs you meet (ch3).

## Good and bad signs

- Good: team members quote the persona when they argue a design choice.
- Good: the persona changed after the last round of interviews.
- Bad: a persona built from averages or pure speculation (ch3).
- Bad: a persona nobody opens after the kickoff (ch3).

## Pitfalls

- Weak personas: missing key facts, badly written, or guessed (ch3).
- Bloated personas full of details that inform no decision (ch3).
- Polishing the first draft instead of treating it as a hypothesis (ch3).
- Writing personas and then no longer talking to customers. New team members
  especially lose touch (ch3).
- Ignoring the buyer in a business sale.

## Contradictions

- [[finding-jobs]] (Competing Against Luck) warns that slicing by customer
  traits finds correlation, not cause, and says to cluster around the job.
  Olsen lists demographic and psychographic segments alongside needs-based
  ones (ch3). Lean on needs-based and behavioral attributes when they
  conflict.
- [[customer-slicing]] (The Mom Test) narrows segments to find people to
  talk to; Olsen's segments define who the product serves. They fit
  together.
