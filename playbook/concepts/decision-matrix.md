# Decision matrix

## What it is

A table of options against shared criteria, also called a Pugh matrix, decision grid or selection grid. Unweighted sums scores; weighted multiplies each score by the criterion's weight; Pugh rates each option +, 0 or − against a baseline such as what you offer today (Asana, Indeed). (see [[2026-11-24-research-digest-decisions]])

## How to use it

- Asana's steps: drop options that fail hard requirements before scoring; choose criteria; agree the evidence for each rating; set weights before seeing scores; rate on a defined 1 to 5 scale where 5 is always best; multiply and total; then check whether a reasonable change to one weight flips the result. (see [[2026-11-24-research-digest-decisions]])
- Record the decision, owner, deadline, hard requirements and scale beforehand; afterwards record evidence per rating, the runner-up and what would change the result (Asana). (see [[2026-11-24-research-digest-decisions]])
- When raters disagree, discuss rather than average (Indeed). (see [[2026-11-24-research-digest-decisions]])
- Treat the top score as a starting point for discussion, not the answer (Indeed). (see [[2026-11-24-research-digest-decisions]])

## Watch out for

- "Do not adjust weights solely to achieve a preferred outcome" (Asana). (see [[2026-11-24-research-digest-decisions]])
- It doesn't remove bias and works only when all options share criteria; use a decision tree for branching choices. (see [[2026-11-24-research-digest-decisions]])
- A CFO quoted by Business News Daily says such tools are better "as communication tools than decision-making tools". (see [[2026-11-24-research-digest-decisions]])

## Contradictions

- Torres says rank ideas by expected impact on one point of intervention, not "complex spreadsheets with half a dozen criteria"; Asana and Indeed recommend weighted multi-criteria matrices. (see [[2026-11-24-research-digest-decisions]])
