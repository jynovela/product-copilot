# Innovation data fallacies

## What it is

Most companies start by solving a job. After launch they drift toward
defining themselves by products, and stop doing the job that brought them
success (Competing Against Luck ch8). Three fallacies about data cause the
drift (ch8):

1. **Active versus passive data.** Before launch, the team lives in passive
   data: the context of the struggle, nonconsumption, workarounds. It is
   quiet and has no champion. After launch, operations create active data:
   sales by product, customer traits, productivity, competitor benchmarks.
   It is loud and precise, so managers start managing the numbers instead
   of the job (ch8).
2. **Surface growth.** Once you have customer relationships, selling them
   more products is cheap and tempting. Doing many jobs for the same
   customers puts you up against focused rivals on each one, and confuses
   customers so they hire the wrong product and fire it (ch8).
3. **Conforming data.** Data bends to support what people already believe.
   Each function brings data that fits its incentives; none of it reflects
   the customer's job. By the time a leader decides, the data has often
   been skewed toward one option (ch8).

## All data is man-made

Someone chose what to collect, how to group it and what to ignore, so all
data carries bias (ch8). Quantitative data hides its bias behind precision;
qualitative data shows its bias openly (ch8). Even financial numbers come
from estimates and negotiation (ch8). Leaders should spend as much time
deciding what data to create as analyzing it (ch8). Stories are rich in
data; numbers distilled from them are broad but shallow (ch10).

## When to use

- Planning growth beyond the core product.
- Reviewing which metrics drive roadmap and investment decisions.
- A once-clear product line has turned into many variants.
- Teams argue from competing data sets.

## Steps

1. Write down the job the company or product was started to do. Check that
   people would describe the business as that job, not as its products
   (ch8).
2. For each key metric, ask: does this track progress on the job, or only
   our operations? (ch8)
3. For each growth idea, ask: does this do the core job better, or is it
   surface growth? Prefer doing the core job better (ch8).
4. Before a decision, ask what data would show you're wrong, and who chose
   the data in front of you (ch8).
5. Keep passive data flowing: field visits, switch interviews, listening to
   support calls (ch5, ch7). One team had all staff spend an hour on real
   support calls, which revealed the job (ch7).
6. Give the job a voice and a champion in resource decisions. Passive data
   needs active management (ch8).

## Good and bad signs

- Good: metrics review starts from customer progress, not product sales.
- Good: someone owns the job and can challenge decisions on its behalf.
- Bad: the product line keeps adding variants with no clear job (ch8).
- Bad: every team's data supports its own plan.

## Pitfalls

- Treating facts as insights and jumping from data to action (ch8).
- Treating big data as truth because it can be computed. Directionally
  useful is not objective (ch8).
- Ignoring that quiet data doesn't change much, because the job doesn't
  (ch8).
