# Net Promoter Score

## What it is

One question, "How likely are you to recommend…?" on a 0 to 10 scale. Promoters answer 9 to 10, passives 7 to 8, detractors 0 to 6; NPS = % promoters − % detractors, a number from −100 to +100, not a percentage (Holmes, HubSpot, Atlassian, Gupta). NPS measures loyalty; CSAT measures satisfaction (Gainsight). NPS is a registered trademark of Bain, Reichheld and Satmetrix (HubSpot). (see [[2026-11-24-research-digest-metrics]])

## How to use it

- Review monthly or quarterly and ask low scorers why (Holmes). (see [[2026-11-24-research-digest-metrics]])
- Survey 7 to 30 days after use (Dovetail). (see [[2026-11-24-research-digest-metrics]])
- Segment it and weight by usage for internal tools (Pendo). (see [[2026-11-24-research-digest-metrics]])

## Watch out for

- Dovetail and Gainsight give the scale as 1 to 10, a likely error; Asana confuses NPS with CSAT; Wrike says NPS includes feature adoption, which is wrong. (see [[2026-11-24-research-digest-metrics]])
- Benchmarks such as "above 50 excellent, above 70 rare" are vendor claims without sources (Dovetail). (see [[2026-11-24-research-digest-metrics]])
- A WorkBoard example OKR moves "NPS from 8.0 to 9.5", which can't be an NPS (see [[2026-11-24-research-digest-goals]]).

## What Lean Product Playbook adds

- NPS is an attitudinal measure of satisfaction and a proxy for product-market fit; customers recommend only products they're very satisfied with (Lean Product Playbook ch13).
- A single wave is only somewhat useful. Track it with periodic surveys, and include an open question asking why they gave the score (ch13).
- Because it reflects overall sentiment, it can flag problems outside the product, such as support (ch13).
- Compare against competitors and category benchmarks (ch13).
