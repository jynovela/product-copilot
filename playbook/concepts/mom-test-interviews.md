# Mom Test interviews

## What it is

Rules for customer questions that produce facts instead of polite lies.
People mislead you to be kind, so getting the truth is the asker's job:
conversations are bad by default until you fix them (Mom Test ch1).

1. Talk about their life, not your idea.
2. Ask about specific past events, not habits, opinions or the future.
3. Talk less and listen more.

They own the problem; you own the solution. They don't tell you what to
build, and you don't tell them what their problem is (ch1).

## When to use

- Learning about a problem before you have anything to sell.
- The opening of any later product or sales meeting: learn first, then
  pitch. A straight pitch gives only yes or no, which is fine for tweaks and
  useless for "does anyone care" (ch5).
- Not enough on its own when the risk is in the product; see Pitfalls.

## Steps

1. Know the three things you must learn from this type of person
   ([[customer-learning-process]]).
2. Open broad unless you're sure the area is a top priority for them: "What
   are your big goals or problems right now?" Zoom in only after they raise
   the topic themselves (ch3).
3. Anchor on a real recent case: "Talk me through the last time that
   happened." Watching them do the task is better still (ch1).
4. Probe cost and effort: what it costs, what they use now, what else they
   tried, whether they ever searched for a fix.
5. Probe money and buying: what they pay today, the budget, whose budget,
   who else can block the purchase.
6. Close with "Who else should I talk to?" and "Is there anything else I
   should have asked?"
7. Reveal the idea only after the facts, then ask for a commitment
   ([[commitment-and-advancement]]).

## Question bank

Ask (ch1):

- "Talk me through the last time that happened." Real behavior; a full
  workflow also shows tools, people, constraints and integrations.
- "Why do you bother?" Moves from the stated problem to the real goal.
- "What are the implications of that?" Separates must-pay problems from
  tolerable annoyances; also a price signal.
- "What else have you tried?" and "How are you dealing with it now?" The
  current fix, its cost (your price anchor), likes, dislikes, switching
  pain. If they never looked for a fix, they won't buy yours.
- "Where does the money come from?" A must in B2B: budget owner, who can
  kill the deal, how buying works.
- "Who else should I talk to?" A refusal means you were pitchy or they
  don't care.
- "Is there anything else I should have asked?" Lets them correct your
  questions; drop it once you know the industry.

Don't ask, or don't trust the answer (ch1):

- "Do you think it's a good idea?" Only the market knows. Learn how they
  solve it now and judge for yourself.
- "Would you buy a product that did X?" or "Would you pay X for Y?" Ask how
  they handle X today and what it costs; if you have something, ask for
  money.
- "How much would you pay?" The number feels rigorous but is still a guess.
  Ask what the problem costs them and what they spend now.
- "What would your dream product do?" Useful only if you then ask why they
  want each thing.

## Good and bad signs

- Good: dates, amounts, named tools; a workaround they built; time or money
  already spent; a strong emotion you then dig into; a flat "meh", which is
  a reliable no (ch3).
- Bad: you talk more than they do; compliments; no question scared you; an
  unexpected answer changed nothing; you can't say which question this
  conversation answers (ch8).

## Pitfalls

- Safe, trivial questions. Every conversation needs one question whose
  answer could sink the current idea (ch3).
- Zooming in too early. Ask a non-exerciser for their biggest gym problem
  and you get a confident answer that means nothing (ch3).
- Ignoring other failure points: confirming the pain but never checking
  the buyer has budget (ch3).
- Product risk. "If you can get me more X, I'll pay" puts the risk on your
  ability to deliver (supply, audience, technology). Talk can't retire that;
  start building sooner. Product risk: can we build it, grow it, will they
  keep using it. Market risk: do they want it, will they pay, are there
  enough of them (ch3).
- Leading questions are fine only to steer to a topic or when you're about
  to end anyway; discount what comes back (ch1, ch3).
- Opinions count only from experts who built something very similar (ch2).

## Contradictions

- Price: The Mom Test says never ask what someone would pay; infer it from
  current spend and budget, or ask for a pre-order (ch1). Van Westendorp
  and Gabor-Granger surveys ask stated price questions directly; see
  [[willingness-to-pay-research]].
