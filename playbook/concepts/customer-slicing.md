# Customer slicing

## What it is

A fuzzy segment gives mixed signals: too many options, no way to prove
yourself wrong, and feedback all over the map. Twenty conversations can
turn out to be one conversation each with twenty kinds of customer
(Mom Test ch7). Slicing keeps splitting a segment until you know exactly
who to talk to and where to find them. Segments for conversations should be
narrower than the segment for the business.

## When to use

- Before a batch of customer conversations.
- After about 10 conversations that still disagree (ch6).
- When every feature debate is won by "those customers would love it".

## Steps

1. Take the broad segment and ask: who in it wants this most? Would all of
   them buy, or only some? Why do they want it? Do all of them share that
   motive? What other motives are there? Who else has these motives?
2. You now have specific groups and a list of motives. Keep slicing the
   generic ones.
3. For each group: what do they already do to reach the goal or cope? Where
   can you find the group? Where can you find people doing that workaround?
4. If you can't say where to find a group, slice it again.
5. From the who-where pairs, start with the one that is most profitable,
   easiest to reach, and most rewarding for you. Pick people you enjoy;
   you'll spend a lot of time with them (ch7).
6. If you already have users, an alternative is to see who likes the
   product most and focus there. An industry expert can give you a
   taxonomy to start from (ch7).

Example: "students" becomes "people scared of public speaking who are
already trying to improve", found at speaking clubs (ch7).

## Talking to the wrong people

Three causes (ch7):

1. The segment is too broad.
2. You missed a segment: two-sided markets, kids and their parents, the
   many groups around a school, or a partner you depend on for making,
   distributing or promoting the product.
3. In B2B, you overlooked people in the buying process.

Talk to people who represent your customers, not the most senior or
impressive ones.

## Good and bad signs

- Good: the same problems and goals keep coming up. With a simple industry
  and a focused segment, that can happen in 3 to 5 conversations (ch6).
- Bad: every conversation brings a new must-have.

## Pitfalls

- A demographic filter ("sales teams of 25 to 250") still hides very
  different needs, tools and goals (ch7).
- You can widen the segment later; starting wide waters down the message
  and invites feature creep (ch7).
