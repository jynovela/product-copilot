# Committed and aspirational goals

## What it is

Google's OKR playbook splits OKRs into committed ones, expected to score 1.0, where a miss needs an explanation and early escalation, and aspirational ones, expected to average about 0.7 with high variance. Aspirational OKRs carry forward until done or deliberately dropped. Some sources add learning goals for discovery. (see [[2026-11-24-research-digest-goals]])

## How to use it

- Label every goal as committed or aspirational; confusing them causes priority inversion (Google's first trap). (see [[2026-11-24-research-digest-goals]])
- Committed examples: meet an SLA, deliver by a date, keep servers within cost (Google). (see [[2026-11-24-research-digest-goals]])
- Teams that depend on another team's stretch goal should expect about 70% delivery, and ask for a committed goal if the dependency is critical (Google re:Work). (see [[2026-11-24-research-digest-goals]])
- Committed goals should use most but not all of a team's resources; committed plus aspirational somewhat more than its resources (Google, against sandbagging). (see [[2026-11-24-research-digest-goals]])
- Read a result by its commitment: an aspirational goal near 70% is not a failure. (see [[2026-11-24-research-digest-goals]])

## Watch out for

- Hit-rate numbers differ because "goal" means different things: Ninety targets 80 to 90% Rock completion, Huryn after Wodtke aims at a 50% chance; Inference: say which kind a goal is before judging it. (see [[2026-11-24-research-digest-goals]])

## Contradictions

- Google balances both types; Digit biases toward committed for nearly everything plus 1 to 2 aspirational. (see [[2026-11-24-research-digest-goals]])
