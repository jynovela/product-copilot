# Integrating around the job

## What it is

Companies organize by function, business unit or region. Successful growth
companies also organize around the customer's job: processes that cut
across functions to deliver the job's experiences every time (Competing
Against Luck ch7). Resources can be bought and products copied; processes
are hard to see and hard to copy, so they are the lasting advantage (ch7).
A process includes formal steps and informal habits, and having no
deliberate process is still a process, just a chaotic one (ch7).

In most companies someone owns each function and product line, but nobody
owns the customer's job (ch7).

## When to use

- Experiences that depend on several teams keep breaking at handoffs.
- A reorg is being planned. Fewer than a third of major reorgs studied
  improved performance; the job lens says fix how teams interact, not the
  boxes on the chart (ch7).
- A mission statement isn't guiding daily decisions (ch9).
- Metrics improve but customers aren't better off.

## Steps

1. Write the job spec ([[job-spec]]) and share it widely.
2. Map the end-to-end experience and every function that touches it.
3. Name a job steward, someone who owns delivering the job end to end, the
   way a coordinator plans a patient's whole visit (ch7).
4. Design processes that shift complexity and hassle from the customer to
   you, such as calling back in minutes and fetching documents for them
   (ch7).
5. Define metrics for the experience elements that matter most to the job,
   not internal efficiency (ch7). See "Metrics" below.
6. Build processes from reusable parts, like code subroutines, and let them
   change when your grasp of the job improves. Stay attached to the job,
   not today's way of solving it (ch7).
7. Use the job as the common language in cross-functional meetings, so
   debate stays on the job rather than opinions and politics (ch7).

## Metrics

- Measure customer benefit, not internal output: response time in minutes,
  delivery date not ship date, time saved for the customer (ch7).
- Each product can need its own set, based on the benefit it delivers (ch7).
- One telling question: would customers hire you again? (ch9)
- Easy metrics (clicks, conversion, retention) roll off servers. The
  important one, whether you improved the customer's life, often has to be
  estimated from surveys and server data (ch9).
- Optimizing a revenue metric can hurt the job: forcing trial users to
  call sales raised upsell revenue but made registering hard (ch7).

## The job as commander's intent

Mission statements are too broad to act on. A clear job tells people at
every level what matters and what they can't compromise on, so they decide
without being told, like a soldier who knows the commander's intent (ch9).
A broad mission can still work if a specific job sits under it (ch9).

A clear job brings four benefits (ch9):

- **Distributed decisions:** people act on their own, in line with the job.
- **Resource focus:** shows what to fund and what to stop.
- **Inspiration:** people see how their work helps someone make progress.
- **Better measurement:** teams look for metrics tied to the job.

It also acts as a filter for what not to do, and helps hire people who
believe in the job (ch9).

## Good and bad signs

- Good: frontline staff make job-aligned calls without asking (ch9).
- Good: you can name who owns each customer job.
- Bad: process changes are judged only on efficiency.
- Bad: a good idea dies because local processes reject it, the
  organization's "immune system" (ch7).

## Pitfalls

- **Stack fallacy:** thinking it's easy to build the layer above your
  technology. Knowing how to make something isn't knowing what customers
  want from it (ch7).
- Optimizing processes that aren't aligned with a job: getting better at
  the wrong thing (ch7).
- Changing the job to fit your processes, instead of the other way round
  (ch7).
- Expecting a reorg to fix it. Silos have value and will stay; integrate
  through processes and metrics instead (ch7).

## Contradictions

- [[team-topologies-model]] organizes teams around streams of change and
  tries to keep cross-team dependencies low. Competing Against Luck keeps
  functions and adds processes that cross them (ch7). Both say structure
  should follow the flow of value, but they differ on whether to change
  the team boundaries.
