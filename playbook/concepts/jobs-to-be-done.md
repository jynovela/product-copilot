# Jobs to be done (JTBD)

## What it is

A job is the progress a person is trying to make in a particular
circumstance. People "hire" a product to get a job done and "fire" it when
something does it better (Competing Against Luck ch2). The theory answers
one causal question: what makes a customer buy and use a product? Customer
traits, product attributes, trends and competitor moves correlate with
buying but don't cause it (ch1, ch2).

A job has four parts (ch2):

- **Progress:** movement toward a goal. A job is a process, rarely one
  event, and it recurs.
- **Circumstance:** where, when, with whom, while doing what, what came
  before and after, plus life stage, family and money. The circumstance is
  the unit of analysis. The same person hires different things in
  different circumstances.
- **Three dimensions:** functional, emotional and social. The emotional and
  social sides often outweigh the functional one, and most teams study only
  the functional side (ch2, ch4).
- **Tradeoffs:** what the person will give up to get the progress.

Every product has competitors outside its category: anything else that
could be hired for the job, including doing nothing (ch2, ch3).

## What isn't a job

- **A need.** "I need to eat" is always true, so it can't explain why
  someone picks one option, or none (ch2).
- **A life theme.** "Be a good parent" guides choices but is too abstract
  to design for. Jobs are the specific situations where you try to make
  progress toward a theme (ch2).
- **An adjective.** "Convenience" may be an experience the job requires,
  but it isn't a job. "If you or a colleague describes a Job to Be Done in
  adjectives and adverbs, it is not a valid job" (ch10). Write jobs as verbs
  and nouns.
- **A product spec.** If only products from one category could do it
  ("a 12-ounce chocolate shake"), go up a level of abstraction. You're at
  the right level when the candidates come from different categories
  (ch10).

## When to use

- Deciding where to innovate and what to build next.
- Defining who you really compete with, and how big the market is.
- Segmenting by situation instead of by demographics.
- Explaining why a product sells but isn't used, or is used in odd ways.

## Steps

1. Find candidate jobs in your own life, in nonconsumption, workarounds,
   things people avoid, and odd uses ([[finding-jobs]]).
2. Interview people who recently switched, and build their story from
   first thought to purchase and use ([[jtbd-switch-interview]]).
3. Map the forces for and against switching, and what got fired
   ([[forces-of-progress]]).
4. Write the job as progress in a circumstance, with all three dimensions
   and the tradeoffs. Check it against "What isn't a job".
5. Turn it into a job spec: competing solutions, obstacles, and the
   experiences of buying and using ([[job-spec]]).
6. Build the processes and metrics that deliver it every time
   ([[integrating-around-the-job]]).

## Good and bad signs

- Good: the job explains a buying pattern that demographics couldn't, such
  as cheap and luxury buyers subscribing at the same rate (ch7).
- Good: competitors you list come from other categories.
- Bad: the job names your product or a feature.
- Bad: the job fits everyone, all the time.

## Pitfalls

- Treating the theory as an ethics test. It explains why people choose
  something, not whether you should offer it (ch1).
- Calling every motive a job. Christensen limits the term on purpose,
  because "disruption" lost its meaning by being stretched (ch10).
- Splitting customers into ever finer slices. Jobs insights come from
  clustering stories into a coherent picture, not from slicing data (ch2).

## What the framework articles say

These are vendor and practitioner articles, not neutral sources.

- Customers "hire" products for a job with functional, emotional and social sides; examples: Intercom, and Christensen's milkshake study. (see [[2024-09-09-product-school-8-pm-frameworks]])
- Focuses on scenarios rather than personas; championed by Clayton Christensen. (see [[neal-cabage-21-pm-frameworks]])
- Shifts the team's focus from the product to what customers want to get done. (see [[productplan-37-pm-frameworks]])
- Reframes the problem around what customers need rather than what they want. (see [[techreviewer-popular-pm-frameworks]])
- Helps you understand why people use a product. (see [[linkedin-10-pm-frameworks-post]])
- The progress a customer is trying to make when hiring a product. (see [[2026-11-20-juan-pm-frameworks-list]])
- A job plus its circumstance; five steps (identify, define, understand circumstances, prioritize, design); break jobs into steps, needs and variables; template "When (situation), I want to (job), so I can (outcome)"; most teams miss the emotional and social jobs. (see [[2023-05-17-hellopm-jobs-to-be-done]])

## Contradictions

- HelloPM's example job statements name solutions, such as "I want to use Spotify..." and "I want to request a reliable ride" (see [[2023-05-17-hellopm-jobs-to-be-done]]). Competing Against Luck says a job that only one product category can do isn't a job (ch10). Keep the solution out of the job.
- TechReviewer frames JTBD as what customers need (see [[techreviewer-popular-pm-frameworks]]). Competing Against Luck says needs are too generic to be jobs (ch2).
- HelloPM's "When..., I want to..., so I can..." template (see [[2023-05-17-hellopm-jobs-to-be-done]]) is the job story format. General knowledge: it came from Intercom and Alan Klement, not Christensen, and Ulwick's job statements use a different format (verb, object, context). The book confirms the drill-and-hole line is Levitt's, who credited Leo McGinneva (Competing Against Luck ch8).
