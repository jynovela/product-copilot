# Customer learning process

## What it is

The routine around customer conversations, so learning reaches the whole
team instead of staying in one head (Mom Test ch8). If one person holds all
the customer knowledge, "the customer said so" becomes a trump card and
nobody can check what was actually said.

## When to use

- Before, during and after every batch of customer conversations.
- When one person "owns" customer learning and the rest of the team just
  hears conclusions.

## Steps

Before a batch:

1. Choose a focused segment you can find ([[customer-slicing]]).
2. With the team, pick the three most important things to learn from this
   type of person. Each type gets its own three. Don't re-ask what you
   already know reliably; pick up where you left off (ch3).
3. Find the scary questions: "If this fails, what killed it?" and "What
   would have to be true for a huge success?" Gut answers are enough (ch8).
4. Decide the commitment and next step you'll ask for, if you're showing
   something ([[commitment-and-advancement]]).
5. Write your best guesses about what this person cares about (up to an
   hour). Answer anything desk research can answer first, and spend 5
   minutes on their company and profile.
6. Minimum prep, if short of time: "What do we want to learn from them?"
   If you don't know, skip the conversation (ch8).

During: frame it, keep it casual, ask questions that pass the Mom Test,
deflect, anchor and dig, take notes, and ask for commitment if relevant
([[mom-test-interviews]], [[interview-bad-data]]).

After a batch:

1. Review notes and exact quotes with the team, plus the craft: which
   questions worked, what you missed.
2. Move notes into permanent shared storage.
3. Update beliefs and plans, and pick the next three questions.

## Who attends

- Everyone making big decisions, tech included, attends some
  conversations (ch8).
- Two people is best: one talks, one takes notes and steps in on bad
  questions. Three or more overwhelms. Solo is fine once you take good
  notes.
- Don't outsource learning to hires: bad news from a hire is hard to
  accept, and hires tend to gloss over lukewarm signals (ch8).

## Notes

- Write exact quotes in quotation marks; otherwise note the idea (ch8).
- Tag each note with a signal: excited, angry, embarrassed; pain, goal,
  obstacle, workaround, background; feature request or buying criterion,
  money or budget, named person or company, follow-up (ch8).
- Keep notes sortable, shareable with the team, permanent, and apart from
  to-dos. One learning per card makes sorting easy. If you can't write
  during the chat, write right after. Recordings tend to go unheard (ch8).
- Inference: in this wiki, notes go to `raw/notes/` and are ingested. The
  pain, goal and workaround tags map onto problem pages; money tags onto
  pricing lines.

## Timing

- An hour of prep, not a week. A week or two of conversations, then give
  them something to commit to. Keep talking for the life of the company
  (ch8).
- Talking to customers is a tool, not a duty. Skip it when it won't help,
  and sometimes just call the customer and ask (ch8).

## Good and bad signs

Bad, meaning you're going through the motions (ch8):

- You talk more than they do, or they compliment you.
- You pitched and don't know what happens next.
- No notes, or notes nobody reviewed with the team.
- An unexpected answer didn't change the idea.
- No question scared you, or you can't name the question you're answering.
