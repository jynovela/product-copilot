# Job spec

## What it is

The job is written from the customer's view. The job spec is the
innovator's view: what must we design, build and deliver so the job gets
done well? (Competing Against Luck ch6) It captures:

- the functional, emotional and social dimensions of the progress
- the tradeoffs the customer will accept
- the full set of competing solutions to beat, including workarounds and
  doing nothing
- the obstacles and anxieties to overcome

The spec is the blueprint. Products succeed because of the experiences they
enable, not their features (ch6).

## Three layers

Solving a job takes three layers, and competitors struggle to copy all
three (ch6):

1. **Uncover the job** ([[jobs-to-be-done]]).
2. **Create the experiences** of purchase and use that the job needs, and
   remove the obstacles.
3. **Integrate around the job**, with processes that deliver those
   experiences every time ([[integrating-around-the-job]]).

Products can be reverse engineered; the experiences and processes behind
them are much harder to copy (ch6, ch7). Removing obstacles is how a product
becomes a service, so every complete solution is in effect a service, even
for product companies (ch6).

## When to use

- After switch interviews, before designing a solution.
- A good product isn't selling: look for obstacles to buying or using.
- Choosing what to add, and what to leave out.

## Steps

1. Restate the job: progress, circumstance, three dimensions.
2. List what customers will trade off, and what they won't.
3. List every competing solution, across categories.
4. List obstacles for each stakeholder, at the Big Hire and the Little Hire
   ([[forces-of-progress]]). Example: a medical device team found four
   barriers to care: low awareness, poor diagnosis, a confusing care path
   and cost. Nonconsumption was the main competitor (ch6).
5. For each obstacle and dimension, design an experience of buying or using
   that answers it.
6. Say who should not hire the product, and why (see below).
7. Check every proposed feature against the spec. Drop what the job doesn't
   need; it's overshooting (ch3, ch6).

## Say who shouldn't hire you

In a world of public reviews, customers who hire you for the wrong job
leave bad reviews. Describe what the product is for, and when not to choose
it, so the wrong customers stay away (ch6).

## Purpose brand

A purpose brand becomes a synonym for the job it does, so customers stop
looking at alternatives (ch6). It is built by doing the job well again and
again, not by advertising (ch6). It:

- commands a premium price, which is the fee customers pay for guidance
  (ch6)
- tells your own designers and marketers which improvements matter and
  which would overshoot (ch6)
- often enters "closed" markets by aligning with a job no incumbent
  prioritized (ch6)

Brands without a clear job become category placeholders that compete on
price (ch6). Adding features for "news" or a price rise can erase a purpose
brand (ch6), as can a line of variants that leaves customers asking what the
brand is for (ch8).

## Premium prices

When a product nails the job, customers often pay more, not less, because
the job matters and alternatives fall short (ch3, ch6). Example: a small
business accounting product with half the features at twice the price led
its market, because it did the job its users had (ch3).

## Good and bad signs

- Good: every major experience traces back to a line in the spec.
- Good: reviews describe the job, not features.
- Bad: the spec reads as a feature list.
- Bad: new features are added because competitors have them.

## Pitfalls

- Stopping at layer 1. Knowing the job doesn't get you hired (ch6).
- Designing only the core product, not buying, setup, support and disposal
  of the old thing (ch5, ch6).
- Writing a product spec instead of a job spec (ch7).
