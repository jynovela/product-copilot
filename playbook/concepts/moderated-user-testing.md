# Moderated user testing

## What it is

Qualitative testing where a moderator watches one target customer use a
design or product and asks questions. Teams get "product blindness": they
know the product too well to see what a new user hits within minutes. User
testing is the cure, and the first round usually surprises most (Lean
Product Playbook ch9). One test can check hypotheses at several layers of
the [[product-market-fit-pyramid]]: needs, value proposition, feature set
and UX (ch9).

## When to use

- Every round of wireframes, mockups or prototypes, before coding (ch7,
  ch9).
- After building the MVP, since the live product may differ from the
  designs (ch10).
- When metrics show what users do but not why.

## Steps

### 1. Choose the format

- **One at a time.** Groups bring fear of judgment, dominant voices and
  groupthink, so focus groups give poor data (ch9).
- **Waves of five to eight.** Enough to find the major issues and see
  patterns. Fix what you learned, then test a new wave (ch9). Schedule one
  extra person per wave, since about 10 percent don't show up (ch9).
- **In person if you can.** You catch sighs, faces and where they look,
  and build rapport. Use moderated remote sessions when users are far away.
  Use unmoderated recorded tests later, when you're more confident; they're
  faster, cheaper and free of moderator influence, but you can't ask "why
  did you click that?" (ch9).

### 2. Recruit the right people

- Write a **screener**: multiple-choice questions with answers that
  qualify or disqualify, based on the persona. Behavioral questions are
  especially useful, such as hours of gaming per week (ch9).
- Revise the screener when a wave reveals a segment that doesn't fit your
  value proposition (ch9).
- Sources: existing customers, local postings linked to a screener survey,
  remote panels that let you set screener questions, events where the
  audience gathers, or research firms. Ad hoc testing in cafés or malls is
  cheap and fast but gives little control over who you get (ch9).
- **Book sessions on a fixed schedule**, such as three users every Tuesday,
  before you know what you'll test. This breaks the trap where recruiting
  starts only when designs are ready, and the feedback arrives after the
  team has moved on (ch9). Give recruiting to someone with a good screener
  (ch9).
- Compensate fairly, in line with the value of the person's time. Access to
  a private beta can work too (ch9).

### 3. Prepare

- Write a test script: what you'll show, the tasks and the questions, in
  order. Pilot it with a teammate (ch9).
- Have users bring their own device where possible (ch9).
- Seat the moderator and a note-taker beside the user. Keep the room to
  three people at most; others watch on a video feed. Watch live; recordings
  rarely get watched (ch9).

### 4. Run about an hour

- **Warm-up and discovery (10 to 15 min).** Build rapport. Say you want
  honest feedback and that criticism helps. Ask them to think aloud. Then
  ask about current behavior, a recent experience end to end, likes,
  dislikes and satisfaction, all before you show your product (ch9).
- **Product feedback (40 to 45 min).** Show the artifact, then step back.
  Let them explore what they find naturally; send them to other parts only
  when needed (ch9).
- **Wrap-up (5 to 10 min).** Ask for 0 to 10 ratings on value, likelihood
  of use and ease of use, perhaps on a short form. Answer their questions
  and pay them (ch9). Add two yes-or-no questions: willing to join future
  research, and want to be told when it launches. A glowing user who says no
  to both was being polite (ch9).

### 5. Ask well

- No leading questions ("that was easy, wasn't it?") (ch9).
- **Echo back** what you saw, then ask: "I see you clicked that button.
  Could you tell me why?" Vary your "why" with "tell me more" (ch9).
- Answer a user's question with a question: "What would you expect to
  happen?" (ch9).
- Ask open questions ("how do you choose a flight?"), not closed ones ("do
  you choose by price?"). Say the question in your head first. Don't tack a
  suggested answer onto an open question, and let silences run (ch9).
- **Don't help.** Don't explain, point or take the mouse. If the artifact is
  too broken to use alone, stop testing and fix it. Never argue with or
  blame the user (ch9).

### 6. Capture and synthesize

- Record each user's feedback, good and bad, in a table with one column per
  user, grouped by **feature set** (right benefits?), **UX** and
  **messaging** (ch9).
- Compute the share of users raising each item and the median ratings
  (ch9).
- Progress across waves shows as more positive items, old complaints
  disappearing (new users never thank you for a fix), and rising ratings
  (ch9).

## Usability is not product-market fit

Ask separately how valuable the product is and how easy it is (ch9). Poor
usability, bugs and unclear messaging hide value early on. Once users sail
through, you still must ask how much they value it; an easy product can
still be one people won't use (ch9). If fit is weak after usability is
fixed, revisit the value proposition and feature set, and also the target
customer (ch9).

## Good and bad signs

- Good: the whole team watches sessions together and shares one picture
  (ch9).
- Good: complaints from wave 1 are absent in wave 2.
- Bad: around one user in ten gives little feedback; more than that points
  to the screener, script or moderator (ch9).

## Pitfalls

- Testing people outside the target market. Their feedback can steer you
  the wrong way (ch9).
- Recruiting only when designs are ready (ch9).
- Rescuing struggling users, which hides the problem (ch9).
- Reading smooth sessions as proof of fit (ch9).

## Contradictions

- [[mom-test-interviews]] warns against asking for ratings or predictions of
  future use. Olsen's wrap-up asks for exactly those, but cross-checks them
  with commitment questions and treats them as rough "semi-quant" data
  (ch9). Weigh behavior and commitments over ratings.
