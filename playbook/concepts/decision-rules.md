# Decision rules and team decision models

## What it is

Roman Pichler's decision rule states who decides and how you can tell the decision is made. His four rules: unanimity (everyone endorses it; strong support, slow), consent (no meaningful objection), majority or supermajority, and the product person decides after discussion (fast, weaker buy-in). MIT's five team models run from least to most participatory: leader decides and informs; leader gathers input then decides; leader sets constraints and delegates; consensus; consensus with a fallback. (see [[2026-11-24-research-digest-decisions]])

## How to use it

- Choose the rule before the discussion; silence after "is everyone OK with this?" isn't agreement (Pichler). (see [[2026-11-24-research-digest-decisions]])
- Decide business-as-usual items yourself; involve stakeholders and the team for strategy and roadmap changes, pivots, new markets, retiring a product or moving dates (Pichler). (see [[2026-11-24-research-digest-decisions]])
- MIT's consensus check: I have heard your positions; you have heard mine; the decision doesn't compromise my values; I can fully support it. (see [[2026-11-24-research-digest-decisions]])
- Limit dominant voices with brainwriting, private votes, the nominal group method or Delphi rounds (NCBI, Atlassian, Zeda). (see [[2026-11-24-research-digest-decisions]])

## Watch out for

- Consensus isn't unanimity: it is full support after a fair hearing (MIT). (see [[2026-11-24-research-digest-decisions]])
- Majority vote suits low-impact decisions but leaves people feeling they lost on important ones (MIT). (see [[2026-11-24-research-digest-decisions]])
- Not the same as an experiment's decision rule, the threshold set before a test. (see [[2026-11-24-research-digest-decisions]])

## Contradictions

- One decider (RAPID, SPADE) vs group rules that include unanimity and majority (Pichler, MIT, Atlassian). (see [[2026-11-24-research-digest-decisions]])
