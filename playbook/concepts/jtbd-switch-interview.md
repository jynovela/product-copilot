# JTBD switch interview

## What it is

Customers can rarely say what they want, and what they say often doesn't
match what they do. They can tell you about their struggles (Competing
Against Luck ch5). A switch interview rebuilds the story of one recent
purchase in fine detail, so you can see the circumstance, the struggle and
the forces at play. Think of yourself as part detective, part documentary
filmmaker (ch5).

## When to use

- After [[finding-jobs]] gives you a candidate job.
- To learn why a product is or isn't bought, or bought and not used.
- Before writing a [[job-spec]].

## Steps

1. Recruit people who recently hired (or fired) a solution in your domain,
   recent enough to remember details.
2. Keep a beginner's mind. Drop what you think you know, so it doesn't
   filter out the clues (ch5).
3. Start at the purchase and work outward. Ask about the day, who was
   there, what else they bought, how they felt (ch5).
4. Ask for the timeline: when did they first think about a change? What
   happened then? What did they try? (ch4, ch5)
5. Dig into the struggle with the old solution: what failed, how often,
   what workarounds they tried (ch5).
6. Ask what nearly stopped them, and what finally tipped them (ch5).
7. Ask who else was involved and what they feared about that person's
   reaction (ch5).
8. Write it as a storyboard (below), and map the forces
   ([[forces-of-progress]]).
9. Repeat until stories cluster. One interview gives hypotheses and new
   questions, not an answer (ch5).

## Storyboard

Use Pixar's story spine to lay out the struggle (ch5):

- Once upon a time...
- Every day...
- One day...
- Because of that, we did this...
- Because of this, we did that...
- Finally I did...

The more realistic the storyboard, the more design decisions it forces:
who, where, how tired, how many bags, how they feel on arrival (ch5).
Mark each emotional moment, including the first Little Hire, the first time
they use it (ch5).

Example: a mattress buyer never mentioned a single mattress feature. The job
was sleeping well enough to be a good spouse and parent. Painkillers and
energy drinks were among the workarounds, so they were competitors too
(ch5).

## What to look for

- Moments of struggle, nagging tradeoffs, imperfect experiences and
  frustrations that recur (ch5).
- What got fired, including "living with it" ([[forces-of-progress]]).
- Barriers at the Big Hire (deciding) and at the Little Hire (using).
- Emotional and social stakes, which customers rarely volunteer.

## Good and bad signs

- Good: the job you find feels true on first hearing. A real insight
  doesn't need convincing (ch5).
- Good: insights suggest a solution beyond anything customers could have
  asked for (ch5).
- Bad: the story is about product features.
- Bad: you heard what the focus group said and stopped there. A focus
  group rejected a product that later sold millions (ch5).

## Pitfalls

- Big samples with few data points each. You need many data points from a
  small sample: depth over breadth (ch5).
- Trusting stated preferences over actions (ch5).
- Collecting facts without building the narrative. Jobs insights are
  stories, and breaking them into binary variables destroys meaning (ch2,
  ch5).

## Contradictions

- [[opportunity-scoring]] (Ulwick's Outcome-Driven Innovation) surveys many
  customers to rate outcomes. Competing Against Luck says insights come
  from depth with few people, not breadth (ch5). Use stories to find the
  job and its outcomes, then surveys to size them, if at all.
