# Forces of progress

## What it is

Two pairs of forces fight over every switch from one solution to another
(Competing Against Luck ch5).

| Force | Direction | What it sounds like |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Push of the situation | toward change | "This keeps going wrong and I'm fed up." |
| Pull of the new solution | toward change | "That would make my life better." |
| Habits of the present | against change | "I'm used to doing it this way." |
| Anxiety of the new | against change | "What if it isn't better?" |

The push has to be big enough to make someone act; a mild annoyance isn't.
The pull of the new must be much greater than habit plus anxiety combined
(ch5). Teams focus on pull (features and benefits) and ignore the two
forces holding people back (ch5). Loss aversion makes the old feel safer
than it is (ch5).

Anxiety takes several forms: about cost, about learning something new,
about the unknown, and about what others will think (ch5). Solutions with
emotional and social sides are harder to fire than purely functional ones
(ch5). This holds in B2B too: a buyer worries about supply failures and
about looking bad to their boss (ch5).

## Big Hire and Little Hire

- **Big Hire:** the moment of purchase. Most data tracks only this.
- **Little Hire:** each moment of actual use. A product that does the job
  gets hired again and again (ch5).

Downloads, sign-ups and sales show Big Hires. Only repeated Little Hires
show the job is done (ch5). Failed Little Hires of the old solution often
drive the switch: people are less eager to hire the new than desperate to
fire the old (ch5). Barriers can sit at either moment: deciding to buy, or
using it after (ch5).

## Something always gets fired

To hire your product, a customer must fire something: another product, a
workaround, or doing nothing (ch5). Ask what yours replaces, not only how to
make it more appealing (ch5).

## When to use

- Analyzing switch interviews ([[jtbd-switch-interview]]).
- A product with strong demand signals but slow adoption.
- High sign-ups but low repeat use.
- Designing onboarding, trials, pricing terms and guarantees.

## Steps

1. For a recent switch, list what pushed, what pulled, what habit held them
   back, and what they feared.
2. Name what got fired, including "living with it".
3. Rate each force. If habit plus anxiety beat pull, more features won't
   help.
4. Design experiences that cut anxiety and habit: free returns, no annual
   lock-in, a physical presence for an online service, a trial run, letting
   both decision makers be present (ch5).
5. Track Little Hires (repeat use), not just Big Hires.

## Good and bad signs

- Good: you can say what each customer fired.
- Good: usage data shows repeat Little Hires, not just purchases.
- Bad: the roadmap is all pull (features) and nothing on anxiety.
- Bad: success is measured in downloads or sign-ups.

## Pitfalls

- Assuming your product is so good that switching friction disappears
  (ch5).
- Missing other characters: a spouse, a team or a boss who must approve
  (ch5).
- Locking customers into contracts, which raises anxiety so they don't join
  (ch5).
