# Minimum viable product (MVP)

## What it is

General knowledge: In Eric Ries's Lean Startup, an MVP is the version of a product that lets a team collect the most validated learning about customers with the least effort. It can be a video, a landing page or a manual service, not only software. It is a learning tool; a small first release is a different thing.

## How to use it

- Name the assumption the MVP tests and the result that would change your mind.
- Pick the cheapest test that can show real behavior, such as a fake door, concierge or video.
- Decide what you'll do with each result before you run it.

## Watch out for

- Calling a cut-down v1 an MVP with no hypothesis.
- An MVP so rough it can't test the real question.

## What the framework articles say

These are vendor and practitioner articles, not neutral sources.

- Just enough features to satisfy early adopters and gather feedback. Examples: Dropbox's demo video, Airbnb renting the founders' own apartment, Zappos photographing shoes in local stores. (see [[2024-09-09-product-school-8-pm-frameworks]])
- Credited to Eric Ries; a bare-bones product to test assumptions and gather feedback; "also called Lean Software Development". (see [[neal-cabage-21-pm-frameworks]])
- The final outcome of prototyping: a product with only the features necessary to launch, so the team launches quicker. (see [[product-development-process-guide]])
- MVPs must solve a real problem; focus on doing one thing well, then use the roadmap to show what comes next. (see [[productplan-37-pm-frameworks]])
- Build the smallest version that achieves your goals; you can always add features later. (see [[techreviewer-popular-pm-frameworks]])
- A good MVP is ugly: it solves the core job badly enough to test demand; build a skateboard, not a quarter of a car. (see [[2026-04-22-hellopm-first-principles]])

## What the research digests say

- The minimum viable experiment or test: the simplest thing that validates one part of an idea (Conversion.com, Ben Smith). (see [[2026-11-24-research-digest-experiments]])
- Huryn places minimal products (piecemeal, concierge, Wizard of Oz) after cheap demand tests and before prototypes; see [[pretotyping]]. (see [[2026-11-24-research-digest-experiments]])
- Strategyzer: a smaller version of the product is a poor test artefact because it adds too many variables. (see [[2026-11-24-research-digest-experiments]])

## Contradictions

- The sources use MVP in two different senses. Product School, Neal Cabage and HelloPM use Ries's meaning: a test for validated learning (see [[2024-09-09-product-school-8-pm-frameworks]], [[neal-cabage-21-pm-frameworks]], [[2026-04-22-hellopm-first-principles]]). The process guide and Techreviewer use it to mean the smallest launchable product (see [[product-development-process-guide]], [[techreviewer-popular-pm-frameworks]]). Ask which one people mean.
- Neal Cabage says MVP is "also called Lean Software Development" (see [[neal-cabage-21-pm-frameworks]]), and ProductPlan says Lean Software Development is "sometimes called the MVP strategy" (see [[productplan-37-pm-frameworks]]). General knowledge: Lean Software Development (Mary and Tom Poppendieck, 2003) is a set of engineering principles taken from lean manufacturing. MVP comes from the Lean Startup. They are different ideas.
