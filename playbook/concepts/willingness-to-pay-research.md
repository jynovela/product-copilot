# Willingness-to-pay research

## What it is

Methods for finding what customers will pay. Van Westendorp's price sensitivity meter asks four questions (too cheap to trust, a bargain, getting expensive, too expensive) to get an acceptable range. Gabor-Granger asks whether people would buy at a series of prices to estimate a demand curve. Conjoint (discrete choice) has people pick between bundles of features and prices and lets you simulate changes. A/B tests show real buyers different prices. Price elasticity is the % change in quantity ÷ the % change in price. (see [[2026-11-24-research-digest-pricing]])

## How to use it

- Pick by stage: conjoint and surveys work before launch; A/B tests need traffic (Inference from the sources). (see [[2026-11-24-research-digest-pricing]])
- Other methods named: monadic tests (each person sees one price), cohort analysis by signup price, localized rollout tests, focus groups and customer interviews. (see [[2026-11-24-research-digest-pricing]])
- Elasticity example (Revology, illustrative): 10,000 units at $20 with a $12 cost. At elasticity −1.5, a 5% rise lifts gross profit 4%; at −3.0 it loses $3,500. (see [[2026-11-24-research-digest-pricing]])
- Gupta's process: set the objective (share or profit), gather data, pick a model and say why the others lost, write a pricing document for each function, then test and iterate. (see [[2026-11-24-research-digest-pricing]])

## Watch out for

- Every camp sells its method: Qualtrics and Sawtooth sell conjoint, Gupta teaches A/B testing. (see [[2026-11-24-research-digest-pricing]])
- Revology warns against assuming linear demand, overcomplex models and black-box AI. (see [[2026-11-24-research-digest-pricing]])

## Contradictions

- Gupta calls pricing-page A/B tests the "gold standard"; Qualtrics and Sawtooth say conjoint is, call Van Westendorp and Gabor-Granger dated, and Sawtooth sees no good reason to use Gabor-Granger. (see [[2026-11-24-research-digest-pricing]])
