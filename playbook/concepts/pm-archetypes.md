# PM archetypes

## What it is

General knowledge: An archetype describes the kind of PM a person is, not the job title: their background, instincts and the work they do best. Adam Fishman names seven: growth, UX-inclined, internal, general manager, optimizer, technician and white-spacer. Alex Mitchell names five: technical, analytic, marketing, get-stuff-done and visionary. Shreyas Doshi describes three leader archetypes: operator, craftsperson and visionary. These are practitioner models, not research.

## How to use it

- Score yourself and ask your manager to score you; compare the two.
- Match archetypes to the type of work: white-spacers for zero-to-one, technicians for scaling, optimizers for core features.
- As a manager, balance archetypes on a team for the work ahead.

## Watch out for

- Using an archetype as a fixed label instead of a starting point for growth.
- Treating a blog model as a validated assessment.

## What Escaping the Build Trap adds

Source note: from secondary summaries of the book, not the book text. Chapter numbers are the book's.

- Perri names three bad PM archetypes, defined by behavior rather than
  background (Build Trap ch6):
  - **Mini-CEO:** acts on authority they don't have and dictates from on
    high, so developers and designers resent them. PMs lead by influence.
  - **Waiter:** takes orders from stakeholders and customers, with no
    vision and weak prioritization. Asks "when" more than "why" and rarely
    pushes back. Ends in David J. Bland's product death cycle, where
    customers are asked to design the solutions.
  - **Former project manager:** project managers are wired for "when", PMs
    for "why". Merging the roles loses the strategic view of customer,
    business and market.
- These are dysfunctions to avoid, unlike the strength-based archetypes
  above, which match people to work. Don't map one set onto the other.
- The great PM works with the team to find the product that meets business
  needs by solving user problems, using research, data, experiments and
  the team's ideas (ch7). They are tech-literate enough for trade-offs and
  market-aware, without needing to be engineers or domain experts (ch7).
  Lone wolves who design solutions alone clash with design and engineering
  (ch7).
- Strong PMs keep asking: why this, why now, what result, what does
  success look like, and what could go wrong (ch7).
- Perri's appendix question "what are your product managers like?" names
  both failure modes: PMs seen as dictators and PMs seen as weak
  order-takers (Build Trap appendix). See [[build-trap]].
