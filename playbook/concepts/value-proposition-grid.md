# Value proposition grid

## What it is

The value proposition names the customer needs your product will meet and
how it will be better and different from the alternatives (Lean Product
Playbook ch5). It is the third layer of the
[[product-market-fit-pyramid]]. Choosing the needs you'll meet also means
choosing the ones you won't, and that is the essence of strategy: deciding
what you're not going to do (ch5).

Olsen's grid uses [[kano-model]] categories, scored against competitors
(ch5):

- **Must-haves** are required, but every product in the category has them,
  so they aren't the core of your value proposition (ch5).
- **Performance benefits** are where products choose to compete. Products
  in one category usually overlap here (ch5).
- **Delighters** often differ between products (ch5).

Your core value proposition is the performance benefits you choose to win
on plus your unique delighters (ch5).

## When to use

- After picking the underserved needs, before choosing features.
- Entering a crowded market, where you need a clear reason to switch.
- Checking whether a planned product is just "me too" (ch5).

## Steps

1. **List the benefits** one per row, grouped as must-haves, performance
   benefits and delighters (ch5). Write delighters as benefits, not feature
   names: "save time entering a query", with the feature in the cell (ch5).
2. **Add a column per competitor and one for you.** Include indirect
   alternatives. Even with no direct rival, customers meet the need somehow,
   for example with pen and paper (ch5).
3. **Score each cell** (ch5):
   - Must-haves: Yes or No.
   - Performance benefits: High, Medium or Low, or real numbers where you
     can measure them, such as the number of restaurants or the time to book
     one.
   - Delighters: one row each, Yes where it applies.
   - For a new product, enter the scores you plan to reach.
4. **Pick your differentiators.** Choose where to play offense and what to
   cede (ch5). The benefits you'll win on should be underserved: high
   importance, low satisfaction ([[opportunity-scoring]]) (ch5).
5. **Add "now" and "later" columns** for you and each competitor, for
   whatever horizon fits your strategy (ch5). Predict where competitors will
   invest and what new delighters they'll launch. Then decide where you'll
   extend a lead, where you'll close a gap and what you'll ignore (ch5).
   Watch for substitutes from outside the category too: a camcorder company
   facing phones that record video (ch5).

### Example shape

| Benefit | Competitor A | Competitor B | You |
| ------- | ------------ | ------------ | --- |
| Must-have 1 | Yes | Yes | Yes |
| Performance 1 | **High** | Low | Medium |
| Performance 2 | Medium | **High** | Low |
| Performance 3 | Low | Medium | **High** |
| Delighter 1 | **Yes** | | |
| Delighter 2 | | | **Yes** |

Bold marks each product's differentiators (ch5).

## Good and bad signs

- Good: the team can name the one performance benefit it will be best at.
- Good: the grid says plainly which benefits you'll be merely acceptable
  on.
- Bad: your column is "High" everywhere. That's a wish, not a strategy.
- Bad: your differentiators are benefits customers don't rate as important.

## Pitfalls

- Trying to meet too many needs. Like a Swiss Army knife with too many
  tools, the product gets heavier and less useful (ch5).
- Competing on a benefit that has stopped mattering. As rivals all became
  good enough on index size and freshness, relevance became the benefit
  that decided the market (ch5).
- Falling short on the important performance benefits while differentiating
  on minor ones. A new entrant must at least match the leader on what
  matters most, then add a valued differentiator (ch5).
- Underestimating switching. People resist leaving a product they use
  routinely, especially in a well-served market, so you need a large surplus
  of value; Olsen recalls the "10×" rule (ch5).
- Treating the grid as a one-time snapshot (ch5).

## Contradictions

- [[value-proposition-canvas]] (Osterwalder) matches customer jobs, pains
  and gains to your products, pain relievers and gain creators, with no
  competitor columns. Olsen's grid is built around competitors and Kano
  categories (ch5). Use the canvas to find fit with one segment and the
  grid to state how you'll beat the alternatives.
