# Finding jobs

## What it is

Job hunting is a way of looking, not a research technique. Surveys,
interviews and observation all work; what matters is the questions you ask
and how you piece the answers into a story (Competing Against Luck ch4).
The first clue is a struggle: a barrier to progress or a frustrating
experience (ch4). Jobs have always existed; only the solutions change, so
any new product must replace something (ch5).

## When to use

- Looking for where to innovate or grow.
- A market looks saturated or commoditized.
- Before interviews, to know what to look for ([[jtbd-switch-interview]]).

## Where to look

1. **Your own life.** Our own unmet jobs are a rich source; many
   breakthroughs began as a founder's own struggle. If you use your
   company's product, ask where it falls short for your jobs (ch4).
2. **Nonconsumption.** People who hire nothing because nothing does the job
   well enough. Most research studies current customers, but nonconsumers
   often show the biggest opportunity, and the market can be much larger
   than the category suggests. Existing data won't show them; you have to
   look (ch3, ch4). Ask what stops them: cost, access, skill, stigma, a
   process too hard to navigate.
3. **Workarounds and compensating behaviors.** People cobbling together
   several products, or bending one to fit. They care enough to invent their
   own fix, so the job is important and they'd value a real solution. You
   only see these when you're immersed in their context (ch4).
4. **Negative jobs.** Things people want to avoid doing, like a trip to the
   doctor to confirm what they already suspect. These are often the best
   opportunities (ch4).
5. **Unusual uses.** People using a product in ways you didn't intend, such
   as baking soda left open in the fridge. Watch how customers use your
   product in the field (ch4).

## Describe it like a documentary

For each candidate, imagine filming someone struggling to make progress.
The film should answer five questions (ch2):

1. What progress are they trying to make? What are its functional,
   emotional and social sides?
2. What is the circumstance? Who, when, where, while doing what?
3. What obstacles get in the way?
4. How are they making do: an imperfect product, a workaround, or nothing?
5. What would "better" mean to them, and what tradeoffs would they accept?

Example: "I see a dentist twice a year, and my teeth still don't look white
enough" is a circumstance; "whitening kits burn and need an overnight mouth
guard" is a compensating behavior (ch2).

## Steps

1. Pick a domain and list struggles from each of the five places.
2. Write a documentary sketch for each, with the five answers.
3. Keep the ones with a strong struggle and a weak current solution.
4. Interview to confirm and enrich them ([[jtbd-switch-interview]]).
5. Write the job and check it isn't a need, a theme or a spec
   ([[jobs-to-be-done]]).

## Good and bad signs

- Good: people are spending time, money or effort on a workaround.
- Good: the people hiring nothing outnumber current customers.
- Bad: every candidate is "a better version" of your current product.
- Bad: you found the job in a spreadsheet, not in anyone's day.

## Pitfalls

- Studying only current customers, so nonconsumers stay invisible (ch4).
- Studying only the functional side of the job (ch4).
- Cutting price when the job isn't mainly functional. A very cheap diaper
  didn't sell until the team saw its emotional and social side: the baby,
  and so the parents, sleeping through the night (ch4).
- Treating one observation as the answer. Jobs come from clustering many
  stories (ch5).

## Contradictions

- [[customer-slicing]] (from The Mom Test) narrows a segment into who-where
  pairs to find people to talk to. Competing Against Luck warns against
  slicing customers into finer segments and says to cluster around the job
  instead (ch2). They work together if you slice by circumstance, not
  demographics.
