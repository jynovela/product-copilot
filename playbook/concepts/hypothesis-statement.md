# Hypothesis statement

## What it is

A testable statement of what you will change, for whom, what you expect to happen to which metric, and why. You never prove it; you try to reject the null hypothesis that the change and the outcome are unrelated (Ben Smith, Scribbr). The independent variable is what you change, the dependent variable what you measure. (see [[2026-11-24-research-digest-experiments]])

## How to use it

- Templates: "We believe that [changing X] for [users Y] will [impact Z] because [reason]" (Product School); "If [segment] gets [change], then [metric] will [move] because [reason]", plus what would falsify it (Product HQ); "At least X% of Y will do Z" for demand tests (Huryn). (see [[2026-11-24-research-digest-experiments]])
- State how much, not "wait and see": "users in the new onboarding reach activation 10% more often" (GrowthBook, LaunchDarkly). (see [[2026-11-24-research-digest-experiments]])
- Write the falsifier: if no result would change the roadmap, "you are decorating a decision already made" (Product HQ). (see [[2026-11-24-research-digest-experiments]])
- One hypothesis per experiment; split competing ones (Conversion.com, Ben Smith). (see [[2026-11-24-research-digest-experiments]])
- Quality test: someone else can read it and explain back what will change and what effect is expected (Simon Cast). (see [[2026-11-24-research-digest-experiments]])

## Watch out for

- Never change the hypothesis after the results are in; that is HARKing (PLOS, Conversion.com). (see [[2026-11-24-research-digest-experiments]])
- Hypotheses from brainstorm notes alone are weak; take them from discovery, support themes, funnel gaps or sales objections (Product HQ). (see [[2026-11-24-research-digest-experiments]])
