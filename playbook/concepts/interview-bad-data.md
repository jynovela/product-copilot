# Bad data in customer conversations

## What it is

Three kinds of answer look like evidence but aren't (Mom Test ch2):

- **Compliments.** "Sounds great." Kind, cost nothing, carry no data.
- **Fluff.** Generic claims ("I usually", "I always", "I never"), future
  promises ("I would", "I will") and maybes ("I might", "I could"). People
  describe who they want to be, not who they are.
- **Ideas and requests.** Features they suggest once they get excited.

They cause false negatives and, worse, false positives. Two habits invite
them: seeking approval (asking for opinions, or showing how much you care
so they protect you) and pitching until they say something nice (ch2).

## When to use

- During any conversation, the moment one of these turns up.
- Reviewing notes, and whenever the team says "everyone loves it".

## Steps

- **Deflect a compliment.** Apologize if you were pitching, then ask how
  they handle the problem today. Treat "cool, keep me posted" as a no.
- **Anchor fluff.** "When did that last happen?" then "Talk me through
  it." Fluffy questions aren't toxic; trusting their answers is. A "do you
  ever" can open the way to a concrete one.
- **Dig beneath a request.** "Why do you want that?" "What would it let you
  do?" "How do you cope without it?" "Should we delay launch for it?" "How
  would it fit your day?" Expect one of three answers: nice to have; a
  must-have every current option lacks (a possible differentiator); or a
  costly home-made workaround (a likely early customer).
  Example: a client asked for analytics, but really wanted a branded weekly
  email to keep their own clients happy, a far simpler product (ch2).
- **Dig beneath emotion.** Anger, embarrassment or joy all deserve "Tell me
  more", "What makes it so bad?", "Why haven't you fixed it yet?"
- **Cut off your pitch.** "Sorry, I slipped into pitch mode. You were
  saying...?" If they want to hear the idea, promise it at the end.
- **Put a flaky claim to a decision.** "Why not download it now?" separates
  a complainer from a customer (ch2).
- **Let them misunderstand.** When they say "So it's like...", don't correct
  them. That's a view of their mental model (ch2).

## Good and bad signs

- Bad, in the room: "Thanks!", "Glad you like it", "No, you don't get it",
  "Yes, but it also does this", "Be honest, I can take it."
- Bad, back at the office: "It went really well", "Lots of positive
  feedback", "Everyone loves it." Test: can you say why they liked it, what
  it saves them, how it fits their life and what else they tried? If not,
  you collected a compliment (ch2).
- Good: a lukewarm "not sure" or "pretty neat". It reliably means they don't
  care. Don't pitch harder. Ask briefly why (not a big deal, not your
  customer, the approach, pitch fatigue, a bad day), thank them and leave
  (ch3).

## Pitfalls

- Asking for honest criticism doesn't stop people protecting you. Famous
  founders who demand harsh feedback get it only because nobody fears
  hurting them (ch2).
- Putting requests straight on the roadmap leads to feature creep.
  Understand requests; don't obey them (ch2).
- "I would definitely buy that" is the most dangerous fluff (ch2, ch5).
