# Finding customer conversations

## What it is

Ways to find people to learn from, and to set up the conversation so it
stays about them (Mom Test ch4, ch6). Keep problem, solution and sales
conversations separate, but the first one doesn't need to be a meeting. A
quick, casual chat learns as much with far less overhead (ch4).

## When to use

- Starting discovery in a new market or segment.
- When conversations are hard to get, or keep turning into sales pitches.

## Steps

1. **Keep it casual where you can.** At an event or by chance, skip "can we
   meet?" and ask your most important question. If it feels like they're
   doing you a favor, it's too formal (ch4).
2. **Go to them.** Cold outreach: the rejection rate doesn't matter; the
   goal is a few conversations that turn into intros. Find a reason to talk
   about a topic you both care about. Spend time where they gather. A
   landing page is useful mainly for the emails it collects, so write to
   every signup (ch6).
3. **Bring them to you.** Host a meetup for the group, teach or speak,
   write about the industry, or host a recurring exchange between respected
   people in it. The host gets credibility and leads (ch6).
4. **Get warm intros.** Ask everyone whether they know someone in the
   group. Advisors with a small stake can make regular intros; professors
   and investors know many people. Follow up on old "let me know how I can
   help" offers (ch6).
5. **Frame a needed meeting** in five parts: vision (the problem you want
   to fix, not your idea), framing (your stage, and that you're not
   selling, if true), weakness (what you can't figure out), pedestal (why
   they in particular can help), ask (for their help). Order can change
   (ch6).
6. **Run it.** Restate the purpose, name who introduced you, and go
   straight to your first question. You set the agenda and propose next
   steps (ch6).

## How long

- 5 minutes to learn whether a problem exists and matters; 10 to 15 for a
  workflow; an hour or more for industry detail (ch4).
- A sales meeting needs 30 minutes or more: about 5 for late arrival, 5 for
  hello, 5 for goals, problems and budget, 10 for the product, and 5 for
  next steps (ch4).

## How many

- Keep going until you stop hearing new things: 3 to 5 conversations for
  a simple industry and a focused segment. More than 10 with scattered
  results means the segment is too fuzzy ([[customer-slicing]], ch6).
- Most customer questions should be answerable within a week (ch6).

## Good and bad signs

- Bad: "Can I interview you?", "Can I get your opinion?", an open "coffee
  sometime?", or "So, um..." at the start. Each signals a boring, needy or
  aimless meeting, and an aimless one becomes a bad sales meeting (ch6).
- Bad: the meeting turns into them grilling you on your idea.

## Pitfalls

- Turning every chance into a calendar block wastes time and misses
  chance conversations (ch4).
- Going in looking for customers feels needy. Go looking for advisors
  instead; it changes how you behave, even if you never say it (ch6).
- Leaning on surveys or calls to dodge the awkwardness of meeting people
  (ch6).

## Contradictions

- Calls: the author prefers meeting in person, because calls lose body
  language, feel scripted and rarely build relationships, though he grants
  some people do well with calls (ch6). Inference: remote video interviews
  are now common in discovery practice; no ingested source covers this yet.
