# Objectives and key results (OKRs)

## What it is

General knowledge: An objective says what you want to achieve; two to five key results say how you'll know you're getting there. Introduced at Intel by Andy Grove.

## How to use it

- Write key results as outcomes, not tasks.
- Keep few, and review them on a regular cadence.
- Pair metrics so one can't be gamed at the cost of another.

## Watch out for

- Key results that are a to-do list.
- Too many objectives.

## What the framework articles say

These are vendor and practitioner articles, not neutral sources.

- Sets measurable objectives and an action plan. (see [[linkedin-10-pm-frameworks-post]])
- Connects high-level objectives to measurable outcomes over a set period. (see [[2026-11-20-juan-pm-frameworks-list]])
- Uses Christina Wodtke's definition: two to five key results per objective. Without a clear product strategy, OKRs don't make sense. Outcomes are changes in behavior; output key results are sometimes fine, especially as leading indicators. Strict cascades push teams toward outputs; quarterly cycles are a habit, not a rule. (see [[2023-01-20-tim-herbig-okrs-in-product-management]])

## Contradictions

- This page says to write key results as outcomes, not tasks. Tim Herbig says output key results are sometimes the better start, warns against a "dogma of outcomes", and prefers action-oriented leading indicators to lagging outcomes that arrive too late (see [[2023-01-20-tim-herbig-okrs-in-product-management]]). Treat outcomes as the aim, not a rule for every key result.
