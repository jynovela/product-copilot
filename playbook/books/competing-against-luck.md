## Core argument

Innovation feels like luck because companies study correlations (customer
traits, product attributes, trends, competitors) instead of the cause of
buying. People hire products to make progress in specific circumstances.
Understand that job, in all its functional, emotional and social detail,
design the experiences that remove obstacles to buying and using, and build
processes around it, and success becomes predictable. Growing companies
drift away from the job because operational data is louder than the
customer's context. (see [[2016-10-04-clayton-christensen-competing-against-luck]])

## What it adds

- The precise definition of a job and tests for what isn't one: needs,
  life themes, adjectives, and product specs.
- Where to look for jobs, and the four forces that decide a switch.
- The Big Hire versus Little Hire split: buying data alone can't show the
  job is done.
- The step from job to job spec to experiences to processes, which other
  JTBD sources mostly skip.
- Why companies lose the job as they grow: three fallacies about data.

Weaker on: interview mechanics (one annotated transcript, no guide or
sample sizes), sizing and prioritizing jobs, and evidence. It is built on
case stories from companies the authors advised or sit on boards of, which
they disclose. It argues stories beat numbers but gives no way to check
when a job is wrong.
