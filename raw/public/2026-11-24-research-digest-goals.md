# Goals: what the research taught

Summary of Juan's goals research (one pasted file, about 75 articles on general goal setting, product goals and OKRs), extracted 2026-11-23 and 2026-11-24 for turning into playbook pages. Everything below is paraphrase unless quoted. Each claim names the article it comes from (full details in section 6). "Vendor" means the publisher sells a tool, course, consulting or training on the topic, so its claims are marketing until checked. Where I add something from model memory it is marked "General knowledge:"; my own reasoning is marked "Inference:".

Left out: the AI search summary in the paste, product pitches, one duplicated article (the Kazinik piece appeared twice), salary content (none appeared), and student, therapy, sport and HR performance-review material except where it carries a reusable method (process/performance/outcome goals, mastery vs performance goals, approach vs avoidance goals, implementation intentions). Off-topic pieces skipped: Sondra Orozco "8 PM skills" (2023-03-14) and a McKinsey AI story.

Decision already taken for product-copilot (Juan, 2026-11-23): goals get their own `goal` page type. In the plan doc, a business goal's Parent is the active strategy page; product goals point to business goals; if no strategy page exists, Parent says "none recorded" and the copilot flags it.

Overall judgement: the research is strong on OKR mechanics (Google's playbook, Herbig, Wodtke via Huryn, Murphy's Tripadvisor process) and on the outcome-vs-output distinction. It is weak on evidence: most statistics are vendor-cited and unsourced, and most vendor example OKRs break the vendor's own "outcomes, not outputs" rule. Treat the examples as illustrations of structure, not as models to copy.

## 1. Frameworks, models, methods and lists

### 1.1 Goal-quality checklists (acronyms)

- **SMART.** The most cited checklist. Letters vary by source:
  - Common: Specific, Measurable, Achievable/Attainable, Relevant, Time-bound (CU Boulder Health, university HR DLCI, "SMART Goals for Effective Business Management", Growthspace, Wrike, Product School, Araujo).
  - Variants: Actionable instead of Achievable, Rewarding instead of Relevant, Time-specific (NSLS); Actionable (Wrike PM guide).
  - Doran's 1981 original: Specific, Measurable, **Assignable** (who does it), Realistic, Time-related ("Goal-Setting Best Practices: Beyond SMART Goals", 2023-12-11; also Perdoo's history).
  - How to use it: as a filter on a goal already drafted, not as a way to generate one (Growthspace; Fechter). Fechter's PM version is the most useful: Measurable means an indicator, baseline, target and data source agreed *before* work starts; Achievable means within the team's authority (not dependent on, say, a pricing change another team owns); Time-bound means a review or decision date, not only a ship date.
  - Limits: static and can cap ambition (FranklinCovey; Productboard "Setting Product Goals"); says nothing about whether the goal is the right one; many "SMART" examples in the research name a solution rather than an outcome (ProductPeople's checkout example; Meegle's "Launch new dashboard to 100% of users by end of Q3").
- **SMARTIE**: Strategic, Measurable, Ambitious, Realistic, Time-bound, Inclusive, Equitable (The Management Center, nonprofit training).
- **SMART+** (PositivePsychology.com): SMART plus extras for therapy settings; off-topic beyond the name.
- **PACT**: Purposeful, Actionable, Continuous, Trackable (NSLS). Output-focused; NSLS notes tracking is not the same as measuring, because tracking does not compare against an outcome.
- **FUN**: Flexibility, Uplifting, Numberless (CU Boulder). Personal-goal style; no reusable PM method.
- **CRAFT**: Clear, Realistic, Aligned, Flexible, Trackable (Growthspace, vendor).
- **HARD**: Heartfelt, Animated, Required, Difficult (Mark Murphy, via CO—/US Chamber "10 goal-setting methodologies").
- **Six W's**: who, what, when, where, why, which resources (NSLS).
- **Locke's five factors** of an effective goal: clarity, challenge, commitment, feedback, task complexity ("Beyond SMART Goals").
- **Doerr's key result criteria**: specific, time-bound, aggressive yet realistic, measurable, verifiable ("Beyond SMART Goals").
- **Seven criteria for an individual goal**: important, authentic (self-concordant, after Sheldon & Elliot), purposeful, intrinsically motivating, clear and measurable, time-bound, challenging but achievable ("Beyond SMART Goals").
- **Three criteria for a set of goals**: highly selective, complementary, prioritized ("Beyond SMART Goals"; links to goal competition, 1.2).

### 1.2 Goal-setting theory and behavioural methods

- **Locke & Latham goal-setting theory.** Specific, challenging goals beat "do your best" goals; time-bound goals beat open-ended ones; individual goals aligned with group goals help team performance. Growthspace describes it as 35 years and 400+ studies; Perdoo quotes an 11–25% performance gain. General knowledge: this is one of the best-replicated findings in organisational psychology, so it is the one evidence base in this batch that is solid, though the exact percentages quoted by vendors are not checked.
- **Stretch-goal risk.** Stretch goals can push unethical behaviour (Blanchard) and "can be crushing if people don't believe they're achievable" (Doerr, via "Beyond SMART Goals"). Wells Fargo and Enron are cited as cautionary tales for single-metric targets (Digit).
- **Implementation intentions** ("if X happens, I will do Y"). Said to make follow-through 2–3x more likely (James Clear, citing a study); also Growthspace, PositivePsychology.com worksheet.
- **Obstacle planning** (WOOP-like): write the future success story, then list obstacles and responses in two columns (Patrick Donadio). General knowledge: WOOP (Wish, Outcome, Obstacle, Plan) is Gabriele Oettingen's method; none of the sources names it.
- **Goals vs systems** (James Clear): the goal sets direction (rudder), the system makes progress (oars). Also: ask "what pain do I want?" (after Mark Manson); goals compete for attention ("four burners"), so drop goals and pursue one at a time; set an upper bound as well as a lower bound; shape the environment; measure to discover, not to judge.
- **Progress Principle** (Amabile & Kramer, via Growthspace): visible small progress drives motivation.
- **Public commitment** raises follow-through (Growthspace).
- **Work backward from the end**; "Goldilocks rule" of difficulty (Donadio); "Begin with the End in Mind" (FranklinCovey, 7 Habits).

### 1.3 Types of goals (reusable classifications)

- **Process vs performance vs outcome goals** (Eva Monsma, sport psychology). Process: how you execute ("bend knees on each of 10 trials"). Performance: your own measurable result ("pass 8 times per scrimmage"). Outcome: the result relative to others ("win"). You control process fully, performance mostly, outcome least. Inference: maps cleanly onto product work as activity / leading indicator / lagging business result.
- **Activity, process, outcome and personal goals** (business.com, Simone Johnson): activity (20 calls a week), process (a new policy or training), outcome (10 referrals; less control), personal. Recommends hybrid process-plus-outcome goals (training programme → fewer cancellations).
- **Mastery vs performance goals** (PositivePsychology.com, after Ryan & Deci): learning a skill vs proving ability.
- **Approach vs avoidance goals** (PositivePsychology.com): move toward something good vs away from something bad. Positive framing is advised (Monsma).
- **Performance vs development goals** (university HR DLCI): doing the job vs growing in it; set jointly by employee and manager.
- **Strategic vs tactical vs operational goals** (Perdoo "Strategic Goal Setting", 2026-11-11). Strategic: 3–5 years, purpose-oriented ("brand awareness +30% in 2 years"). Tactical: medium-term, departmental ("+60% social followers"). Operational: daily. The common trap is setting a tactical goal as if it were strategic. Productboard also splits strategic vs tactical.
- **Business goal categories**: financial, growth, customer, operational, employee, sustainability (Asana business goals); SUU lists six similar categories.
- **Product goal types**:
  - Aha!: business-driven (+20% revenue in 12 months), market-driven (#1 rated app), customer-driven (trial time-to-value 10 days within 6 months).
  - Plane: growth, adoption, retention, customer experience, technical, business (plus strategic); and a second list: outcome, performance, learning, strategic.
  - Pichler: user goals vs business goals (see 1.4).
- **Magnitude vs zero-to-one goals** (BCV, Noah Breslow): growing something that exists vs creating something new. Zero-to-one goals use a "two-step": launch, then scale.
- **Goals by product stage** (Meegle, vendor): pre-product-market fit (validate problem-solution fit; activation, churn), growth (reach and engagement; WAU, feature usage, NPS), mature (optimise and expand revenue; retention, ARPU, support tickets).
- **Learning goals** for discovery: "validate or reject an assumption with 5 users, with a documented decision, by Friday" (Fechter); discovery goals are measured by validated learning plus a recorded decision. Also Plane's "learning" type; Dotwork treats short-term goals as hypothesis tests.

### 1.4 Goal hierarchies and long-horizon systems

- **Vision → strategy → objectives** (Product School, 2024-01-03): vision 3–5+ years, strategy 1–3 years, objectives a quarter to a year. Vision questions include "how will we know that we won?".
- **Product School hierarchy** (2025-04-01): vision > strategy > roadmap > discovery > product goals > delivery > backlog and sprints. Odd placement: it puts product goals *after* discovery and roadmap, where most sources put goals before the roadmap.
- **Pyramid of clarity** (Asana): mission > strategic goals > objectives > key results. Atlassian draws a similar pyramid (vision = why; OKRs = what and how).
- **Pichler's four connected goals** (Roman Pichler, 2023-10-17):
  1. Product vision: a slogan-like ultimate purpose ("healthy eating"), a BHAG, 5–10 years, not measurable.
  2. User goals and business goals: the benefit to users (reduce type-2 diabetes risk) and to the business (diversify, new revenue stream, brand). Specific enough to choose KPIs.
  3. Product goals: each a step toward a user or business goal, reachable in 2–3 months (engagement, revenue, cost, technical debt).
  4. Sprint goals: 2–4 weeks, outcome-framed (e.g. learn whether users will share personal data).
  Links run both ways: repeatedly missed sprint goals should change the product goal. Artefacts: Product Vision Board (Needs, Business Goals), GO product roadmap (a Goal row), sprint goal template. Pichler is "not a fan" of OKRs in product management.
- **Product goal in Scrum** (Scrum Guide 2020, via Product School 2025): the product goal sits at the top of the product backlog, is mid-term, and answers three questions: what are we doing in the next few months, how does it contribute to business objectives, what does success look like and how is it measured. The product owner owns it, after co-creating it.
- **Productboard levels**: company ("$5M ARR and NPS 50+"), product ("activation 30%→50% in 2 quarters"), feature or initiative ("first-week churn −20% via guided onboarding").
- **Goal trees** (Productboard): draw goals as a tree to spot gaps and duplicates.
- **BHAG** (Big Hairy Audacious Goal; Collins & Porras): 10–25 years (Adobe Workfront; "Beyond SMART Goals"; Pichler treats the vision as a BHAG).
- **Ninety / EOS chain**: CAGs (Compelling and Audacious Goals, 5–15 or 10 years; examples Starbucks, Amazon, Microsoft, Stanford) → 3-year goals → 1-year goals → quarterly **Rocks** (90-day priorities). Rocks come from Covey's rocks/gravel/sand/water story and Gino Wickman's EOS. Meeting cadence annual, quarterly, weekly. Rock template: What, Who, Why, How. Vendor claims: "research suggests a 15% failure rate is ideal" (citing Yerkes-Dodson), great companies complete at least 80% of Rocks, Ninety targets 90%.
- **4DX, the 4 Disciplines of Execution** (FranklinCovey, vendor): (1) a Wildly Important Goal (WIG), the single most important goal; (2) act on lead measures, not only lag measures; (3) keep a compelling scoreboard; (4) a cadence of accountability (weekly check-ins). Goal formula "From X to Y by When". Also "5 Choices": act on the important, not the urgent.
- **MBO, Management by Objectives** (Drucker, 1954, *The Practice of Management*): managers review organisation goals, then agree annual objectives with each employee, cascading down, with periodic reviews. Risks: top-down, static, bureaucratic (Adobe Workfront, Perdoo, Meegle, Atlassian). OKRs are described as Grove's refinement of MBO that added key results and made it more collaborative (Wrike, Atlassian).
- **Balanced Scorecard** (Kaplan & Norton): four perspectives, financial, customer, internal process, learning and growth (HBS Online, Perdoo, SUU). HBS test: "could I infer the strategy from the scorecard?"
- **HBS strategy execution tools** (Esther Han, promoting Robert Simons' course): strategy map; profit plan built from three questions; find critical performance variables by imagining how the strategy could fail.
- **Hoshin Kanri** (a matrix linking long-term goals to annual objectives and actions) and **OGSM** (Objectives, Goals, Strategies, Measures): named by Perdoo without detail.
- **Goal decomposition**: break an annual target into monthly, weekly and daily activity numbers. Example: $300k a year → $25k a month → 10 meetings a week → 13 calls a day (Funding For Good; CO—/US Chamber "5 tips", Christine Gomolka).
- **Goals, Signals, Measures** (Atlassian Team Playbook): a template pairing each goal with observable signals and measures.
- **BCV planning model** (Bain Capital Ventures, Noah Breslow, 2023-10-24):
  - Goal horizon depends on company stage: pre-revenue companies plan around a single objective (MVP plus first loan); later, a quarter is too short and 6 months is the right window; annual plans arrive at hundreds of millions in revenue.
  - Inputs: North Star, strategy, constraints (including an explicit out-of-bounds list).
  - Outputs: goals or OKRs, a financial plan, accountability assigned to individuals, not departments (RACI).
  - Bottom-up plans reconciled by leadership; one plan for board and management with core, stretch and downside scenarios; roll out with context; check in every other month.

### 1.5 Goal-setting processes (step lists)

- **The Management Center, 7 steps**: state headlines; apply SMARTIE; write a messy first draft with placeholders; ask for input using MOCHA (manager, owner, consulted, helper, approver); cascade, connect and check that goals add up; finalise; track with monthly or quarterly step-backs. 3–5 goals.
- **Adobe Workfront, 8 steps**: executive input, 1-year goals, break into quarters, make them SMART, no more than 3–5 OKRs, write examples, cascade down and align up, measure.
- **Growthspace rollout, 5 steps**: audit current goals, pick one framework, build manager capability, set an accountability cadence, measure goal quality. Review quarterly at minimum, monthly better.
- **Perdoo strategic goals, 5 steps**: vision, deadline, resources, accountability, review.
- **Product School, 6 steps** (2024): align with vision, make SMART, prioritise with an Eisenhower matrix, set milestones, collaborate, monitor and adapt.
- **Plane, 7 steps**: name the problem or need, define the outcome, pick the metric, set the timeframe (quarter, sprints, release or half), align to strategy, validate with engineering, design and stakeholders, write it in plain language.
- **Fechter, 6 steps** (Product HQ): problem; outcome owner (a team); baseline (definition, date and source); target plus guardrail; review date; list of assumptions. A goal explains the roadmap but does not freeze the solution.
- **Atlassian alignment**: vision → strategic goals → cascade → cross-functional goals → track and adjust. Example: retention +25% in 6 months split into executive, product, engineering, customer success and marketing pieces with a biweekly sync. Warns against teams optimising their own metric at the company's expense.
- **Productboard best practices**: anchor goals in business impact; ambitious but realistic; co-create across functions; keep visible weekly; change goals only in structured reviews (quarterly or at a pivot), not reactively.

### 1.6 OKRs: core structure

- **Definition.** An Objective is a qualitative, inspiring statement of where you want to go; Key Results are a few measurable results that show whether you got there (nearly every OKR source).
- **Grove's two questions** (High Output Management, via Google re:Work): "Where do I want to go?" gives the objective; "How will I pace myself to see if I am getting there?" gives the key results.
- **Doerr's formula**: "I will [Objective] as measured by [Key Results]" (Asana template, Atlassian, Kanban Zone, Araujo). Variants: "We will meet [objective] as measured by [3–5 results]" (Brev); "We need to achieve __ that's measured by __" (Wrike); Viva Goals extends it to "… via [Initiatives]".
- **Wodtke's definition** (Radical Focus, via Herbig and Huryn): an Objective is like a mission statement for a shorter period; it inspires, is hard but not impossible in the time frame, and can be done by the team independently. Key Results quantify it, by asking "How would we know if we met our Objective? What numbers would change?"
- **Third and fourth components**: **Initiatives** (projects and tasks that should move the KRs; outputs, within the team's control) and **weekly check-ins** (Weekdone, TCGen, Perdoo, Viva Goals, Asana "tasks"). Keep initiatives separate from KRs so finishing a task never marks a KR as achieved (Asana, Weekdone, Perdoo).
- **Writing formulas** (Viva Goals, retired product): Objective = verb + what + "so that / in order to" (e.g. "Deliver an essential product in order to delight customers and grow our user base"). KR = verb + what you track + "from X to Y" (NPS 40→50). Initiative = verb + what you do.
- **KR anatomy** (unnamed interview-prep site, 2026-11-21; Asana template):
  - a precise metric unit (exact cohort and event),
  - a baseline and target written "from X to Y",
  - a percentile instead of an average where slow tails cause churn (p80, p95),
  - a hard date and one named owner who runs the weekly check-in,
  - a guardrail that must hold,
  - (Asana adds) a measurement source: which report, which period, which records count.
  - Rule of thumb: "If a draft Key Result reads like a task you can check off on a Tuesday … move it back to your sprint backlog."
- **Objective rules** (Huryn, from Wodtke): one sentence; inspiring; time-limited (Parkinson's Law; usually three months, longer in big companies); owned by one team that can act without much help. Bad objectives are a predefined output ("Implement the new onboarding flow according to the specification") or a metric ("CSAT ≥ 70", which belongs in a KR).
- **Objective rules** (Google re:Work and playbook): avoid "keep", "maintain", "continue"; use endpoints or states; tangible and unambiguous enough that an observer can tell whether it was achieved; fits on one line; promises clear business value. (Re:Work also lists "ship feature Y" as a good endpoint, which conflicts with the outcome rule; see section 4.)
- **Objective qualities** (Perdoo): directional, ambitious yet realistic, understandable, no metric, no jargon, aligned (to strategy, another OKR or a KPI), time-bound. Objective types: build, improve, innovate.
- **KR rules** (Google, Toast, Perdoo, Atlassian, Kazinik):
  - outcomes, not activities; words like "consult, help, analyze, participate" signal activities (Google);
  - one metric, gradable at the end state (Toast);
  - challenging, not business as usual (Toast);
  - within the team's circle of influence (Perdoo; Herbig);
  - measurable often enough to review in the quarter (Atlassian); measurable within 24 hours of setting it, so no "X" placeholder lingers (Surridge);
  - evidence of completion that is available, credible and easy to find (Google);
  - Kazinik's four test questions: is it a number? an outcome, not an activity? ambitious but achievable? does achieving it prove progress toward the objective?
- **Binary and launch KRs.** Some KRs are 0-or-1 ("Launch major product initiative Y"). Productboard says Doerr accepts this form; Digit allows "ship KRs" sparingly when no baseline exists, paired with counter-metrics; Google rewrites them into impact ("Double fleet-wide Y by launching X to 90+% of cells").
- **Counts.** See section 4: sources disagree on the number of objectives and KRs.

### 1.7 OKR types

- **Committed vs aspirational** (Google playbook): committed OKRs are expected to score 1.0, and a miss needs an explanation and an early escalation; aspirational OKRs are expected to average about 0.7 with high variance. Committed examples: meet an SLA, deliver a feature by a date, keep servers within cost. Aspirational OKRs carry forward from quarter to quarter until done or dropped deliberately, and can move to another team. Also Brev, Digit, Productboard (label committed ones clearly), Surridge ("stretch KRs vs high-integrity commitments").
- **Learning OKRs**: a third type for discovery and exploration (Atlassian; monday.com). Herbig's discovery OKRs (1.10) are the most worked-out version.
- **monday.com's six types**: committed, aspirational, learning, top-down, bottom-up, personal (mixes type with direction of setting).
- **Personal OKRs** (Digit): set by doing a self-review written 6 months ahead; outcomes, not "participate in". Productboard suggests one personal-growth objective.
- **Shared or cross-functional OKRs**: one objective, each team owns a KR. Example "Delight users with instant support": product owns an 80% self-service help centre, support owns 95% of tickets resolved in 2 hours, engineering owns 99.9% uptime of support systems (Kazinik). Google: cross-team OKRs appear in each group's OKRs; horizontal OKRs need supporting KRs in each sub-team.

### 1.8 OKR scoring

- **Google scale**: 0.0–1.0 per KR; the objective score is a rough average, sometimes weighted; sweet spot 0.6–0.7; consistently scoring 1.0 means the OKRs are not ambitious enough; low grades are data, not failure (re:Work; playbook).
- **Formulas** (Asana template):
  - increasing KR: score = (current − start) ÷ (target − start);
  - decreasing KR: score = (start − current) ÷ (start − target);
  - cap the result between 0 and 1.
  - For a committed KR, 1.0 means reached and 0.7 means not reached.
- **Bands**: 0–0.3 minimal, 0.4–0.6 partial, 0.7–1.0 significant (monday.com); 0.3 / 0.7 / 1.0 checkpoints, 0.7 = success (Atlassian).
- **Define hit, partial and miss in advance** (Asana tips).
- **Confidence scoring**: record your confidence of hitting each KR and update it at check-ins (Productboard; Airtable).
- **Problem with equal-weight averages**: an average can hide misses. Asana's worked example averages to 0.917 while two KRs missed; airfocus calls equal-weight averaging "failure-focused" and demotivating. Fix: keep actual values visible, weight KRs by importance, score against real targets.
- **Use scores to start conversations, not to judge people** (Kazinik; Google).

### 1.9 OKR traps, tests and anti-patterns

- **Google's six traps** (playbook, reprinted on whatmatters.com):
  1. Not separating committed from aspirational (causes priority inversion).
  2. Business-as-usual OKRs (written from what the team would do anyway).
  3. Timid aspirational OKRs (litmus: does it meet what customers really want?).
  4. Sandbagging (committed OKRs should use most but not all of the team's resources; committed plus aspirational should be somewhat more than its resources).
  5. Low-value objectives (litmus: could it score 1.0 without any end-user or economic benefit? "Increase task CPU utilisation 3%" → "decrease cores needed at peak by 3% with no quality change").
  6. Insufficient KRs for committed objectives (litmus: could every KR score 1.0 and the intent still be missed?).
- **Re:Work's five traps** (same list minus #1, plus "miscommunicating stretch OKRs"): teams depending on a stretch OKR should expect about 70% delivery, and should ask for a committed OKR if the dependency is critical. BAU test: stack-rank current and new work by value vs effort; OKRs should hold only the top items.
- **Necessity and sufficiency tests** (Viva Goals): is every KR needed? Do the KRs together achieve the objective? Digit's version: KRs must be exhaustive. Its example: feature X DAU target 100→200, with every funnel KR green but usage flat, because the real problem was at the top of the funnel. Put the intended outcome in the objective or first KR.
- **airfocus work-plan test** (Lucid case study): "If a key result can be satisfied by doing a task, regardless of the impact, it is a work plan. If it can only be satisfied by evidence that the desired outcome occurred, it is an OKR."
- **Google litmus tests**: if you can write the OKRs in five minutes, they are not good; KRs in team-internal terms ("Launch Foo 4.1") are bad, write the user effect ("Improve sign-ups 25%"); use real dates, not all the last day of the quarter; unambiguous metrics ("1M users": all-time or 7-day actives?); OKRs should cover a significant share of the team's effort.
- **airfocus five pitfalls**: OKRs written after the roadmap is fixed; disguised work plans; equal-weight scoring; team OKRs disconnected from strategy; OKRs as quarterly documentation that nobody opens until scoring week.
- **Kazinik's seven mistakes** (monday.com): too many objectives; task lists as KRs ("conduct user interviews" vs "increase NPS by 15 points"); unambitious goals; ignoring dependencies; skipping reviews; vanity metrics; forcing top-down OKRs.
- **monday.com mistakes** (Trovato): unrealistic, only top-down, insufficient resources, vague, untracked, OKRs as daily tasks, only aspirational or only operational.
- **Murphy's "done wrong" list**: OKRs as a project list, a laundry list of ambitions, a P&L summary, a lofty mission statement, or done once and forgotten.
- **Feature broker vs product dictator** (Murphy): a broker lets everyone vote on features (the CEO gets two votes) and gets "Product Soup"; a dictator imposes a vision and risks being wrong without buy-in. Aim between.
- **OKR Theater** (Herbig): running OKRs without knowing what benefit you expect from them.
- **Invisible OKRs** (Brev): output-only, too many, or ownerless.
- **Surridge's lessons** (12 plus 2): OKRs are no substitute for strategy; run them across the organisation, not only product and tech; need CEO buy-in; cannot change culture alone; outcomes over outputs; separate stretch KRs from commitments; make KRs measurable within 24 hours; use balancing metrics; don't cover everything (IT laptop provisioning needs no OKR); prefer leading metrics; leave room for other work (maintenance, security); keep process to action at about 1:99; but don't have no process; don't fixate on tracking software (a simple, ideally automated, dashboard or spreadsheet works).
- **Digit's ten tips**: big, motivating objectives; KRs with metric, start, target and date ("Reduce AWS cost per server $100→$80 by end of quarter"); binary KRs sparingly; every KR needs a dashboard, with a proxy (1-day retention for 30-day) or forecast when the real metric is slow; KRs must be exhaustive; pair metrics with counter-metrics; committed vs aspirational (the author biases to committed for nearly everything plus 1–2 aspirational); cascade up, down and sideways (teams can refuse, which pushes change upward; settles in 1–2 weeks); personal OKRs; few, focused OKRs, and list what you chose not to do. Bonus: using OKRs well takes years. Also lists Doerr's four "superpowers": focus and commit, align and connect, track for accountability, stretch.
- **Wodtke's five reasons we don't get things done** (via Huryn): goals not prioritised; goal not communicated obsessively; no plan (the original OKR was only a goal format, so add check-ins, celebrations and commitments); no time made for important-but-not-urgent work; giving up instead of iterating.

### 1.10 OKRs in product management

- **Herbig's three attributes of effective OKRs** (Tim Herbig, 2023-01-20):
  1. Rooted in proven evidence about the behaviour change needed, or explicitly exploratory.
  2. Relevant to everyday decisions. KRs that only move in hindsight or sit outside the team's influence are useless. OKRs need not cover all work.
  3. Clearly connected to company priorities, through company OKRs or strategy.
- **Product Strategy-Metrics Sandwich** (Herbig): long-term North Star metrics on top, strategy choices in the middle, quarterly OKRs underneath that test whether the choices work. Strategy choices fall into three patterns: Strategic Narrative (vision, principles, mid-to-long-term goals), Playing Field (market, audiences and their jobs, alternatives) and Winning Moves (distribution, value propositions, offerings, differentiation). For each choice, ask "what would success look like?" to get KRs.
- **Inputs to OKR drafting** (Herbig; TCGen repeats the list): company or department OKRs; purpose and vision; product vision and strategy; previous OKRs and the theme-based roadmap; discovery insights (proven problems, validated solutions). Herbig: without a clear product strategy "it does not make sense to use OKRs".
- **Start with the WHY of OKRs** (Herbig): what organisation do we want, why OKRs, what do we want them to do for product? Then a minimal OKR system: pilot team? facilitator? decision process (bottom-up, team-lead veto, top-down)? which work is covered? fitted into existing routines and tools?
- **Drafting workshop** (Herbig): a facilitator leads; the owners of inputs attend; you may start from KRs rather than forcing objective-first drafting.
- **Holistic KR metrics** (Herbig; TCGen): conversion; NPS or similar; G2 or App Store reviews; onboarding completion; changes in sales-team behaviour (fewer follow-up calls or demo steps); user test results; churn and retention.
- **Balanced KRs** (Wodtke via Herbig; Productboard): include opposing forces (growth and performance, revenue and quality, quantity and quality, adoption and engagement). Herbig's example: "We are an integrated and mature platform solution": enterprise satisfaction > 75%; no sales pitch lost because of "immaturity of the product"; 100% of interviewees associate the site with enterprise, trustworthy and capable.
- **"How might we" check** (Herbig): rephrase a KR as "How might we…?" to catch premature feature talk.
- **Leading indicators for KRs** (Herbig): ask "Which customer behaviors do we need to encourage to achieve our business goal?" Lagging metrics (revenue, growth) move too late to steer by. Action-oriented KRs are acceptable if they let the team make choices instead of waiting.
- **OKRs and roadmaps**: feature-based roadmaps block outcome OKRs; theme-based Now/Next/Later roadmaps fit, with Now mapped to the current cycle (Herbig). Don't turn KRs into Gantt charts. The roadmap answers which work is most likely to create the outcome, so write OKRs before the roadmap is fixed (airfocus). Outcome roadmap item: "Increase user collaboration by 40% in Q2" instead of "Launch chat feature in Q2" (Kazinik). Araujo's flow runs the other way round: OKRs first, then a stack-ranked feature list (accurate for 3 months, rough for 6, a ">6 months" bucket).
- **Discovery OKRs** (Herbig). Timing problem: a 6-week discovery inside a quarter leaves little time, and shipped features need weeks to change behaviour. Two options:
  - process KRs as leading indicators: number of user interactions (an output), share of quantitative vs qualitative research, cycle time of testing ideas with users;
  - user behaviours that predict success: usability-test completion, customer effort score in prototype tests, email open and click rates, beta opt-in and opt-out rates.
  - Consider shorter cycles in discovery or stealth. The main job of a discovery OKR is to make discovery work visible.
- **OKRs and Scrum/agile**: link as many backlog items as possible to an OKR, for example with a Jira custom field (Herbig); prioritise sprint work by which tasks advance current OKRs; map epics to objectives and ask stakeholders "Which OKR does this support?" (Kazinik); keep goals fixed and tactics flexible.
- **Feature-to-outcome rewrite, 4 steps** (interview-prep site): what user behaviour changes a month after launch? why is it stuck today? which metric measures that bottleneck (a driver tree)? attach one guardrail. Example rows: "Ship 5-step onboarding wizard by May 15" → "cut p80 time-to-first-published-project from 6 days to 1.5 days by June 30", guardrail "Day-30 retention of activated teams ≥ 78%"; "Add AI chatbot to help center" → "self-serve resolution without human handoff 31%→54%", guardrail "post-chat CSAT ≥ 4.3/5".
- **Weekdone derivation pattern**: observation (early users cannot find features) → problem (drop-off, low adoption) → KRs (users logging in 3+ times within 10 days 7%→20%; leaders using dashboards within 7 days 5%→25%; HR admins using pulse surveys within 7 days 15%→35%) → candidate initiatives per KR.
- **Kazinik's 5 steps** (monday.com): start with user outcomes (avoid "refactor codebase" unless users feel it); define measurable KRs; set stretch goals (70%); align with business objectives ("expand internationally" → "enable multi-language support"); validate with engineering, design, marketing and sales (do they understand it, can we measure it, what support is needed, is it ambitious enough?).
- **When to use OKRs** (Kazinik): launching new products, scaling the team, changing direction, breaking silos, improving focus.
- **Focus areas for product OKRs**: user research, activation, retention, internal processes (Weekdone); launch, user research, development and design, release management, engagement and satisfaction, operations and enablement (WorkBoard); separate goals per development stage (Businessmap).
- **WorkBoard's rule**: every product OKR should trace back to a customer outcome or a company priority; "If it isn't, it's a task, not an objective." Set product OKRs with customer success, sales and marketing.

### 1.11 OKR process, cadence and rollout

- **Murphy's Tripadvisor process** (Gerard Murphy with Chris Herron; 6 weeks, quarterly within an annual cycle):
  1. Management Guidance: a paragraph or two of plain text on the kinds of outcomes wanted (acquisition or retention, innovation or optimisation, which funnel stage), often with directional time allocations ("70% on retention at first invoice"). Deliberately not written as an objective, so teams write their own. Dropped once teams matured.
  2. Brainstorming with the wider group (PM, engineering, design, research, product marketing, sales, support): objectives first, then ideas; get a feel for what is achievable (is a 10% move realistic?); cull weak ideas.
  3. One Pagers: a mini business case with a rough impact estimate from light data (e.g. 90% of trial users see onboarding but only 10% act, so there is room to improve). Engineering adds a T-shirt size. Anyone can write one, including support staff, and unchosen authors get feedback.
  4. Finalisation: fund the highest-impact One Pagers; balance known outcomes with bigger swings; fit them into available engineering weeks ("play Tetris"); realign KRs once projects are known.
  Plus: cross-functional checkpoints throughout (a sales OKR that needs marketing collateral must be agreed before the quarter); an OKR leaders' Slack room and weekly draft docs; escalate blocking dependencies. Share widely (team leaders' meeting, objectives only at the monthly all-hands, full detail in Slack and email). Changes are expected but rare; when a new opportunity appears (say a 50%-likely $1M deal), weigh it against current KRs and cut something explicitly ("if we do Project 1, we won't have time for Project 2"). End each quarter with a retrospective: did projects ship without results, or did KRs move without projects shipping?
- **Murphy's milestone-story KRs**: research onboarding elements by Feb 1; launch and test the new flow by March 1; raise trial conversion from 35% to 45%. Mixes output milestones with one outcome (contrast with outcome-only advice; see section 4).
- **Google re:Work rollout**: explain what, why, how, and leave time for skeptics; commit organisational objectives first; not every company OKR needs a team OKR, but each team OKR should connect to at least one. Team leaders check their priorities: do they connect to company KRs? make company success more likely? is anything missing? are there more than three?
- **Herbig**: plan an alignment period before the quarter so teams don't "rush and commit"; cycle length should let KRs visibly move without being unpredictable (a quarter is common, not a rule; adjust for B2B vs B2C or the fiscal year).
- **Dränert**: start with a 3-month company cycle and three 1-month department cycles, then move to annual and quarterly; start with leadership; show company OKR progress at least biweekly at the all-hands; celebrate, including instructive failures; not every team member needs OKRs.
- **Rollout patterns**: pilot team (Herbig, Kanban Zone); all at once, top-down, or department by department, with an OKR Ambassador (Perdoo); an "OKR champion" like a Six Sigma black belt (unnamed vendor).
- **Mix of top-down and bottom-up**: each team or individual writes at least 40% of its own OKRs (unnamed vendor); objectives can bubble up from frontline context (Productboard); forced top-down OKRs fail (Kazinik; Dränert, who reports about 50% of people with prior OKR experience had a negative one where KPIs were cascaded to each employee).
- **Check-in formats**: weekly team check-in of 15–20 minutes asking what moved, what is at risk and what to prioritise (Brev; unnamed vendor ≤ 20 minutes); investigate if a KR has not moved after week 1 (Brev); three questions: progress, blockers, help needed (Kazinik); Asana check-in fields: review date and current value, status and reason, next action, owner and next review, final result and lesson; review individual OKRs in 1:1s, team OKRs weekly, organisation OKRs quarterly (Kanban Zone); mid-quarter check-in before final grading (re:Work).
- **Annual plus quarterly layers**: annual OKRs set direction and strategy, quarterly OKRs implement it (Murphy, Perdoo, Atlassian with 3–4 annual company objectives, Google).
- **Visibility**: make OKRs public inside the company (Google); post them where you see them daily (Productboard).
- **OKRs kept separate from performance reviews** so people don't sandbag (Google, Asana, Perdoo, Productboard, unnamed vendor). Toast does the opposite (section 4).

### 1.12 Metrics inside goals: leading, lagging, guardrails, percentiles

- **Leading vs lagging indicators**: lead measures predict and can be influenced now; lag measures confirm later (4DX; Herbig; Aakash Gupta; Atlassian prefers leading). Proxies when the real metric is slow: ARPU for LTV, day-7 or day-30 for longer retention, NPS for growth (ProductPeople); 1-day retention for 30-day (Digit).
- **Counter-metrics / guardrails / health metrics**: pair every growth KR with one that must not degrade (Digit; Fechter; ProductPeople; interview-prep site; Productboard; Surridge; Huryn's "pairing indicators and health metrics"). Fechter's examples of harmful optimisation: support deflection that hides problems, low-quality sign-ups, retention that is really cancellation friction.
- **Percentiles over averages** where tails drive churn (interview-prep site; see the LedgerPulse example in section 5).
- **North Star and input metrics** (Huryn; Product School suggests a North Star Metric when many OKRs are too complex; Herbig's sandwich).
- **No baseline?** Guess one; by quarter end you will know more (Huryn). Digit: use a ship KR plus counter-metrics.
- **KPIs vs OKRs**: see section 2.

### 1.13 Goal decision rules from the PM sources

- Goal = what + when + success metric (Aha!).
- A strong goal has metric + baseline + target + timeframe and does not prescribe the solution ("Increase onboarding completion from 52% to 70% by end of Q3" vs "Improve the product experience"; Plane).
- Good-goal qualities: clear, outcome-focused, measurable, time-bound, aligned to strategy, useful for decisions (Plane).
- Mistakes: vague; goal = feature ("Launch a reporting dashboard"); too many goals; no metric; disconnected from planning; never revisited (Plane). Productboard adds: metric misalignment (e.g. delivery speed as the goal), conflicting priorities, mismatched time horizons, siloed ownership, no visibility, reactive goal churn, underestimated dependencies.
- Traditional failures (ProductPeople): no link to business KPIs, output metrics, ignored dependencies, timelines that skip discovery.
- Ethics (ProductPeople): no manipulative growth tactics, accessibility, avoiding burnout.
- Percentage of roadmap items tied to business goals as a health metric (Meegle).

## 2. Definitions and vocabulary

- **Goal vs objective.** No agreement. The sources split three ways:
  - Goal = broad, qualitative, long-term destination; objective = specific, measurable step or milestone toward it (Asana business goals: "NPS +15 in 12 months" is an objective; SUU MBA page).
  - Objectives = the *actions* each team takes toward a goal (Indeed employer: goal "conversion 20%→25% Jan–Apr"; objectives "merchandising, promotions").
  - HBS Online: objectives are how you get there, goals are where you end up.
  - OKR usage reverses the first meaning: the Objective is the qualitative part and the Key Results carry the numbers.
  - Inference: the copilot should pick one vocabulary (Juan's goal page type) and not import "objective" as a separate level.
- **Outcome.** A change in human behaviour that creates impact (Herbig, after Josh Seiden's *Outcomes Over Output*). Most PM sources mean customer or user behaviour. TCGen instead calls it "the behavioral change in your employees", which is an outlier. Businessmap calls outcomes the "why" behind actions.
- **Output.** An artefact delivered by activities, such as a feature or report (Herbig); "quantities of finished work" (Businessmap). One possible way to cause an outcome.
- **Impact.** The business or mission effect of outcomes (Herbig, Seiden).
- **Objective (OKR).** Qualitative, inspiring, time-bound destination, no metric (nearly all OKR sources). Kazinik breaks this with "Reduce customer churn by 20%" as an objective.
- **Key Result.** A measurable result showing progress to the objective; usually 2–5. Some sources call them "often quantitative goals/KPIs" (Productboard).
- **Initiative.** Work (projects, experiments, tasks) intended to move a KR; an output (Weekdone, Perdoo, Viva Goals, TCGen). Timebook uses "initiative" for a broad theme and "project" for tactical work; Aha! uses "product initiatives" for broad themes of work under a goal.
- **KPI.** An ongoing health metric you monitor and keep within range, versus an OKR, which drives a step change in a time box (Atlassian, Perdoo "keep the lights on", Kazinik, Tiago Rodrigo "car dashboard" vs OKR "GPS", TCGen, interview-prep site using the US Census management survey list of operational KPIs). A KPI that drifts or becomes critical can be promoted to a KR for a cycle (Rodrigo: time to value 30 → 5 minutes; interview-prep site). Whether KPIs carry targets is disputed (section 4).
- **Committed / aspirational / learning OKR.** See 1.7.
- **Leading / lagging indicator; guardrail / counter-metric / health metric.** See 1.12.
- **Product goal.** Several definitions: a measurable, time-bound objective supporting the vision (Aha!); a strategic commitment connecting vision to outcomes (Productboard); a step toward a user or business goal reachable in 2–3 months (Pichler); the Scrum Guide's single mid-term target at the top of the backlog (Product School); a clear, outcome-oriented target in a timeframe (Plane).
- **Sprint goal.** A 2–4-week, outcome-framed objective serving a product goal (Pichler).
- **Business goal / user goal.** Pichler's pair under the vision; business = what the company gains, user = what the user gains.
- **Strategic / tactical / operational goal.** See 1.3 (Perdoo).
- **WIG, lead measure, lag measure.** 4DX terms (FranklinCovey).
- **Rock.** A 90-day priority in EOS and Ninety. **CAG.** Ninety's 10-year goal. **BHAG.** Collins & Porras' 10–25-year goal.
- **MBO.** Drucker's Management by Objectives.
- **Process goal** (Disciplined Agile / PMI): a description of a process area to improve, such as "Product Management" with decision points (monitor the market, evolve the vision, prioritise, evolve the roadmap). Not an outcome goal despite the name.
- **Vision vs mission.** Huryn, Pichler and Roger Martin (per Huryn) prefer a single term; Wodtke says "mission". Most other sources keep both.
- **Feature factory.** Shipping features without checking impact (Productboard).
- **OKR Theater.** Herbig's term for ritual OKRs with no clear purpose.
- **NCTs** (Narratives, Commitments, Tasks): named by Herbig as an alternative goal framework. General knowledge: proposed by Ravi Mehta.

## 3. Practices most sources agree on

1. **Goals describe outcomes, not outputs.** Write what should change for users or the business, not what you will ship. Keep the work (initiatives) separate from the measure (Herbig, Google, Asana, Perdoo, Weekdone, Plane, airfocus, Kazinik, Fechter, Aakash Gupta, WorkBoard, Productboard, nearly all others). Most sources also admit outputs are sometimes acceptable (see section 4).
2. **Few goals.** Focus beats breadth; "if everything is important, nothing is" (Wodtke, Lencioni, Productboard). The common ceiling is 3–5 objectives with 2–5 KRs each, though the exact number is disputed.
3. **Baseline, target and date.** A measurable goal states where you start, where you aim to be and by when ("From X to Y by When": FranklinCovey, Digit, Plane, interview-prep site, Asana template, Viva Goals).
4. **One owner per goal or KR** (Asana, Brev, interview-prep site, BCV "individuals, not departments", Fechter).
5. **Ambitious but believable.** Stretch helps (Locke & Latham) but goals people think are impossible backfire (Doerr; Blanchard).
6. **Regular check-ins during the cycle**, not only at the end. "An OKR revisited only at the end scores history" (airfocus). Weekly or biweekly is the common advice.
7. **Connect every team goal to a higher goal or strategy**, so people can see how their work contributes (Google, Herbig, airfocus, Atlassian, WorkBoard, Productboard, Kazinik).
8. **Mix top-down and bottom-up.** Leadership sets direction; teams write their own goals (Google, Murphy, Productboard, Dränert, unnamed vendor, Perdoo).
9. **Strategy comes first.** OKRs or goals are not a strategy and cannot replace one (Herbig, Surridge, Digit, Huryn, Businessmap, Kazinik).
10. **Keep goals separate from individual performance ratings** (Google, Asana, Perdoo, Productboard, unnamed vendor), with Toast as the exception.
11. **Pair growth metrics with guardrails** so a goal cannot be gamed (Digit, Fechter, Productboard, Huryn, Surridge, ProductPeople, interview-prep site).
12. **Prefer leading indicators the team can move within the cycle**, while keeping the lagging business result in view (Herbig, Atlassian, Surridge, 4DX, Aakash Gupta, WorkBoard).
13. **Goals don't need to cover all work.** Maintenance, security and business as usual run alongside (Herbig, Surridge, Weekdone, Google).
14. **Change goals only deliberately**, in a structured review or with an explicit trade-off, not reactively (Productboard, Murphy).
15. **Review and learn at the end of each cycle** (Murphy, TCGen, Brev's four end-of-cycle questions, Google postmortems for missed committed OKRs).

## 4. Conflicts between sources

- **How many goals?**
  - 1–2 per team per quarter (Brev); a single clear direction per planning horizon (Plane); one goal at a time (Wodtke via Huryn, James Clear); one WIG (4DX); BCV's single objective for pre-revenue companies.
  - 1–3 objectives per team (Weekdone); up to 3 per PM, up to 5 per PM team (Araujo); ≤ 3 objectives (Airtable).
  - 3–5 (Google, Asana-type vendors, Kazinik, Kanban Zone, Adobe Workfront, The Management Center, Aha!, Businessmap, ProductPeople, Productboard).
  - Up to 5 company OKRs a year (unnamed vendor); 3–4 annual company objectives (Atlassian).
- **How many KRs per objective?** About 3 (Google, Huryn); 2–4 (Kazinik); 2–5 (Wodtke via Herbig, Atlassian, TCGen, Kanban Zone); 3–4 (Weekdone); 3–5 (Araujo, Toast, Productboard, Viva Goals, unnamed vendor, Weekdone elsewhere); ≤ 5 (Perdoo, TCGen).
- **What hit rate means success?**
  - Aspirational: 0.6–0.7 (Google), about 70% (Asana, Kazinik, monday.com, Atlassian, SUU citing Deloitte).
  - About 50% chance of success when working hard (Huryn, after Wodtke).
  - 80–90% (Ninety: great companies complete ≥ 80% of Rocks, Ninety targets 90%; "15% failure is ideal").
  - Committed: 1.0 (Google, Brev, Digit, Productboard).
  - Inference: the numbers differ because "goal" means different things. Committed goals should be hit; stretch goals should not be hit every time. A goal page should say which kind it is.
- **Outcome-only vs outputs allowed.**
  - Strict: KRs must be outcomes (Google's rules, Perdoo, Kazinik, airfocus's test, WorkBoard's rule).
  - Pragmatic: outputs or launch KRs are fine sometimes, especially when starting out, when there is no baseline, or for milestone stories (Herbig, Businessmap, Digit's "ship KRs", Productboard citing Doerr, Murphy's milestone KRs).
  - And Google's own re:Work page lists "ship feature Y" as a good endpoint, contradicting its outcome rule.
- **Cascading.**
  - Strict cascade: a higher level's KRs become the next level's objectives (Productboard, Deloitte via SUU, Digit, unnamed vendor "CEO's KRs may become VPs' objectives", TCGen).
  - Loose: Herbig says strict cascades push teams to outputs; use "weak links, not strong ties" and as few cascade levels as possible. TCGen also warns against cascading to the bottom of the org chart. Businessmap shows a cascade diagram credited to Herbig, who argues against strict cascades.
  - airfocus favours an explicit connected hierarchy (team → group → department → ARR target), which is linking rather than cascading.
- **OKRs in performance reviews.** Keep them out (Google, Asana, Perdoo, Productboard, unnamed vendor) vs include them (Toast's internal process). Kanban Zone reviews individual OKRs in 1:1s, which sits between.
- **Check-in cadence.** Weekly (Weekdone, TCGen, Brev, Perdoo, 4DX, Productboard); weekly or biweekly (Herbig, Asana template, Kanban Zone, Kazinik); weekly, fortnightly or monthly (Wrike); monthly (airfocus/Lucid; Dränert's starter department cycles); every other month for company plans (BCV); a few times a quarter (Google re:Work).
- **Cycle length.** A quarter is the norm (most OKR sources); Herbig says it is not a rule; BCV says a quarter is too short for later-stage companies and 6 months fits; shorter cycles in discovery (Herbig); Pichler's product goals run 2–3 months; Araujo says objectives are long-term and shouldn't change month to month, while Kazinik says OKRs change quarterly.
- **Committed vs aspirational balance.** Google balances both and warns against confusing them; Digit biases toward committed for nearly everything plus 1–2 aspirational.
- **Use OKRs in product management at all?** Herbig, Huryn, Murphy, Kazinik and the vendors say yes; Pichler is "not a fan" and prefers his four goal types; Herbig says they make no sense without a clear product strategy.
- **Do KPIs have targets?** KPIs must be hit reliably (Kazinik) vs KPIs "don't necessarily have targets" and are for monitoring (Tiago Rodrigo).
- **Draft objective first?** Conventional advice is objective first, then KRs; Herbig says start wherever ideas come, including KRs first; Huryn (after Torres) suggests brainstorming about 20 KR candidates individually first.
- **Equal-weight average scoring.** Standard Google practice (rough average) vs airfocus and Asana warnings that averaging hides misses.
- **Goal vs objective vocabulary.** See section 2.
- **History of OKRs.** Grove at Intel in the 1970s (monday.com, General knowledge); "first introduced by Andy Grove… in the 1980s" (TCGen); Doerr to Google in 1999 (Perdoo) vs "early 2000" (Google re:Work). TCGen also says Bill Gates implemented OKRs, which no other source supports (General knowledge: Gates endorsed Doerr's book).
- **Statistic conflicts.** The "Dominican University" goal study is quoted as 42% more likely to achieve written goals (FranklinCovey, The Management Center), 30% more (Donadio) and 40% more with weekly accountability reports (Perdoo). General knowledge: this is Gail Matthews' small study, often misquoted.

## 5. Examples and numbers, with flags

### 5.1 Worked examples

- **LedgerPulse p80 and guardrail** (interview-prep site, invented data, arithmetic checks out):
  - 500 sign-ups a quarter; 200 send a first paid invoice within 14 days, so activation is 40%.
  - Of those 200: 150 solo users take 1 day (150 account-days), 50 multi-seat teams take 13 days (650 account-days). The average is 800 ÷ 200 = 4.0 days and hides the problem. The 80th-percentile account (account 160 of 200) takes 13 days, so a p80 KR forces the team to fix multi-seat tax-ID checks.
  - Sizing: $200 a month ($2,400 a year) per retained account; Day-30 retention 80% gives 160 retained accounts. Raising activation to 55% gives 275 activated (+75); at 80% retention that is +60 accounts, $144,000 new ARR.
  - Gaming: a $1 self-invoice prompt hits 55% activation but retention falls to 56%, so 275 × 0.56 = 154 retained, 6 fewer than the 160 baseline. A Day-30 retention guardrail ≥ 80% blocks the shortcut.
- **Asana support-team OKR, Q1 2027** (vendor, invented): reopened tickets 12%→6%, flagged at risk in week 6; an audit found missing escalation guidance; satisfaction reached 89 vs target 90. Equal-weight average 0.917 while two KRs missed.
- **Digit exhaustive-KR failure** (practitioner): feature X DAU 100→200; all funnel KRs green, usage flat; the problem was top of funnel.
- **Digit counter-metric set**: feature X reaches 100 users, 7-day retention 90%, P0/P1 bugs closed within a day.
- **Murphy One Pager**: 90% of trial users view onboarding, 10% act → onboarding is a candidate project for trial-to-paid conversion.
- **Weekdone Example 4 derivation**: see 1.10.
- **Goal decomposition**: $300k/year → $25k/month → 10 meetings/week → 13 calls/day (Funding For Good; CO—).
- **Google rewrites**: "Increase task CPU utilisation 3%" → "decrease cores needed at peak by 3% with no change in quality"; "Launch X" → "Double fleet-wide Y by launching X to 90+% of cells"; "Launch Foo 4.1" → "Improve sign-ups 25%"; "assess latency" → "publish latency measurements from six Colossus cells by March 7".
- **Lucid case study** (airfocus, vendor): department objective progress toward an ARR target at 90%, read in two minutes; monthly check-ins replaced end-of-quarter reconstruction. Quotes from Zack Rowley (Technical Project Manager at Lucid); "ten times better" is a testimonial.

- Productboard company OKR: 20 enterprise deals; sales-touched revenue +20%; ARPA +15%; plus a binary launch KR.

- Pichler: vision "healthy eating" → user goal "reduce type-2 diabetes risk" → product goals → sprint goal "learn whether users will share personal info".

- WorkBoard: "Complete 30 customer development interviews", "Conduct 2 training sessions with 95% attendance", "Publish 4 articles", book club every 6 weeks, release celebration with 5 public recognitions. Also "NPS from 8.0 to 9.5", which can't be an NPS (NPS runs −100 to +100; probably a 0–10 score mislabelled), and "reduce lead time by 7 weeks" with no baseline.

### 5.4 Statistics (all unverified unless noted)

- Locke & Latham: 400+ studies; 11–25% performance gain (Growthspace, Perdoo). Underlying theory is solid (General knowledge); the percentage is vendor-quoted.
- Clear goals → 12–15% better performance (Blanchard, vendor). Gallup: 50% don't know what is expected of them (Blanchard).
- "Specific and challenging goals lead to higher performance 90% of the time" (FranklinCovey; study unnamed).
