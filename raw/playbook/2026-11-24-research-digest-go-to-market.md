## 1. What a go-to-market strategy is

### Definitions in the sources

- **A plan for how a product is positioned, priced, promoted and distributed to a target audience** [PMA]. It brings product, marketing, sales and customer insight together into one value proposition and action plan for launching and scaling a new product. It covers competitive positioning, ideal customer profile, distribution channels, promotion and sales enablement.
- **"A plan that defines how a product reaches its target audience and converts demand into revenue"** [Pragmatic]. It connects product, marketing and sales.
- **"A go-to-market strategy connects your product to a real market problem in a way that drives measurable business results"** [Pragmatic, who say this is more useful than "a plan for launching a product"].
- **A cross-functional plan for launching a product or feature, entering a new market, or reaching a new segment** [Dovetail]. It answers: who is the customer, what problem does it solve, how is it distributed and sold, what does it cost, and how is it positioned.
- **"The way your company turns a product bet into real, measurable outcomes in the market"** [Product School]. Not a launch checklist or a marketing calendar. Four questions: who are we targeting now and why; what problem we solve and why our product is the best way; how we will reach, convert and support them; how we will know it works and when we change course.
- **A step-by-step plan for introducing a new product to buyers** [Salesforce], focused on reaching prospects and driving revenue.
- **The process of introducing a product or service into the market efficiently and effectively** [Kaizen], promoting and selling a solution to a specific audience through the right channels while trying to beat competitors.
- **"Your blueprint for launching a product and winning market share fast"** [Airtable].
- **The plan and processes a company uses to bring offerings to market and reach target customers**, covering all activities, tactics and channels to promote, sell and deliver [Tripp].
- **An "operating system"** that aligns product, sales, marketing, intelligence and customer experience toward entering the market with low risk and high traction [AEVO].
- **Go-to-market model:** a plan for how a company promotes a product to gain customer interest and a competitive advantage [PMA].

### GTM strategy vs related plans

| Compared with | How the sources separate them |
| --- | --- |
| Marketing strategy | GTM is for launching one specific thing (a product, feature or market entry); marketing strategy is the ongoing plan for brand presence and demand across all products [PMA, Dovetail, Airtable]. PMA's table: GTM is narrow (one product, launch or market entry), finite (tied to a launch date and a window after it), and aims for "a successful launch with strong adoption and minimal wasted spend"; marketing strategy is broad, ongoing (revisited quarterly or annually) and aims for brand awareness, pipeline and customer lifetime value. A GTM strategy "usually sits inside a marketing strategy"; you run several GTM strategies over one marketing strategy [PMA]. |
| Marketing plan | A marketing plan is the concrete action plan for a marketing strategy. A GTM strategy can include a marketing plan, but a marketing plan doesn't include a GTM strategy [Coursera]. Airtable: a marketing plan covers brand, demand generation and retention long after launch day. |
| Product strategy | Product strategy says what the product will do and why. GTM sits between product and marketing, translating the product's value into a plan for reaching and winning specific customers [Dovetail]. |
| Business plan | A business plan covers every aspect of a business; GTM covers delivering one product or service to customers [Hinge, Salesforce]. Business plans are written when starting a company or raising money; GTM strategies for launches [Salesforce]. A GTM strategy can be part of a business plan [Salesforce]. |
| Launch plan | Product School and AEVO say GTM is more than a launch plan; Dovetail treats the launch plan as one component of GTM: the sequence of what is announced, when and to whom. |

### When a GTM strategy is needed

- **Product School:** not for every small UI tweak. Needed for bets that change who you serve, how you sell or how you make money: a new product or major module; a new market or segment; repositioning for a different use case; a new pricing or AI monetization model; a major feature with its own adoption and revenue expectations.
- **PMA (quoted by Info-Tech):** also for acquiring businesses, changing business focus, announcing a new feature, entering a new market, rebranding, and positioning or repositioning.
- **Kaizen and PMA:** new product in an existing market, existing product in a new market, new product in a new market. PMA adds testing a new product's market for growth.
- **Salesforce:** every new product or service, plus relaunches, changed products and new audiences. Start when development gets the official go-ahead. Example: a data storage company that served banks creates a GTM strategy when it adds healthcare customers.
- **Coursera:** any launch, including a new iteration or a rebrand.

Inference: Product School's list is the only one that sets a threshold (skip small changes). The others imply a GTM plan for every launch.

## 2. Frameworks, models and lists

### 2.1 PMA's five essential elements [PMA]

1. **Market definition:** which markets you target.
2. **Customers:** the target audience and its demographics in those markets.
3. **Distribution model:** how the product reaches the customer.
4. **Product messaging and positioning:** what is sold and its unique value or main difference from alternatives.
5. **Price:** what it costs for each customer group.

Use: PMA's organizing checklist; its Oatly example (2.11) is mapped onto these five. Limit: no success metrics, ownership or enablement in the five, though PMA covers them elsewhere in the same guide.

### 2.2 The 4 Ps of go-to-market [Coursera]

Product, price, place (distribution), promotion. Coursera calls them "the four Ps of go-to-market strategy". General knowledge: this is the marketing mix from McCarthy (1960), usually taught as a marketing framework, not a GTM one.

### 2.3 GTM components lists

Sources list similar parts with different groupings:

| Source | Components |
| --- | --- |
| Pragmatic (FAQ) | Problem definition, target personas, positioning, messaging, channels, sales enablement, success metrics |
| Dovetail | Target market and segment (ideal company profile and roles in B2B); value proposition and positioning; pricing and packaging; distribution and sales motion; messaging and content; launch plan |
| Product School | 1 market and customer definition (ICPs, personas, problems, jobs and triggers); 2 positioning, value proposition and narrative; 3 pricing, packaging and commercial model; 4 channels and GTM motion; 5 revenue and success metrics; 6 operating model and enablement |
| Salesforce | Product or service description; target audience; value proposition and messaging; sales and marketing channels; sales enablement; success measures |
| AEVO | Target market and demand pockets; value proposition and differentiation; pricing and revenue model; distribution channels; sales architecture; positioning and messaging; customer experience from awareness to renewal; scalability and growth levers |
| Airtable template | Goals per segment; initiatives per goal; executive sponsors; timeline views; status; initiative lead; project docs; collaboration; automations; integrations (a task tracker, not a strategy outline) |
| Info-Tech "essentials" | Product, marketing and sales alignment; buyer personas and journeys; market opportunity size; competitively differentiated product hypothesis; buyer-validated commercial concept; sales revenue plan and program cost budget; business case for build and launch |

Practice nearly all agree on: who, what problem, why us vs alternatives, price, how it reaches them, what we say, sales readiness, how we measure success.

### 2.4 GTM process sequences

- **PMA's 7-step checklist:** (1) identify the buyer and the buying center; (2) build personas and messaging for each role; (3) test messaging before committing budget, and move budget away from channels that don't gain traction after a fair test; (4) map the buyer's journey (awareness, consideration, decision; marketing owns awareness, sales takes over at decision); (5) build the strategy, answering which channel tested best, which messages landed, when a prospect counts as a lead, how prospects want to be contacted, what helps sales close faster, and which assets each funnel stage still needs; set a timeline; (6) create content; (7) set success metrics before launch and adjust if they aren't met.
- **Product School's 8-step framework:** (1) align on objectives and choose the GTM model; (2) define ICPs, personas and segments; (3) map market and competition, including substitutes and non-consumption, ending in "we will win by being the best option for this specific problem, for this specific group, against these specific alternatives, and here is the evidence"; (4) positioning and messaging; (5) pricing, packaging and trials; (6) channel strategy and campaigns, with what good looks like, owner and measure per channel; (7) orchestrate the launch in rollout waves (2.8); (8) measure, learn and iterate.
- **Info-Tech's three phases (17 steps):**
  - Phase 1, establish market truth and strategic focus: form a steering committee and GTM team; define the target problem and buying context; identify and prioritize target buyers; assess competitive and alternative options; size the obtainable market. Outcomes: validated ICP and buying group, problem statements and priority use cases, alternatives and gaps, a realistic opportunity assessment, and a "decision to proceed, pivot, or stop".
  - Phase 2, design value proposition and GTM approach: define the core value proposition; design the offer (product, packaging, pricing); select the GTM motion and routes to market; map the buyer journey; build the business case; align stakeholders. Outcomes include an initial business case and a "commitment to fund and prepare for launch".
  - Phase 3, create the launch plan: finalize launch scope and success criteria; develop a working plan; enable customer-facing teams; align systems, tools and processes; build the launch campaign; secure executive approval and readiness sign-off. Outcomes include defined launch metrics and governance and "executive sign-off to launch and scale".
- **Kaizen's six steps:** create the GTM team; define the target audience (set a numeric target first, study the ICP, quantify total available market, segmented market and market share, analyze competition); develop the value proposition; develop the message; generate demand; define pricing.
- **Hinge's five steps (professional services):** define target markets (start from problems or markets, not services); profile the target client; position the brand against competitors (innovator, thought leader, low-price option); define service offerings, which "should flow from the target and positioning, not the other way around"; develop a matching marketing strategy. Research: secondary research for sizing markets, brand research for the other steps.
- **Coursera's six steps:** identify the target market (segmentation, buyer personas, B2B or B2C); clarify the value proposition (with competitor analysis); define pricing (costs, profit, competitor prices, willingness to pay, subscription or transactional); craft the promotion strategy (channels, inbound vs outbound, budget); choose sales and distribution channels; set metrics and monitor.
- **Salesforce's seven steps:** create buyer personas (start from existing customers in the CRM, interview them, review quarterly); competitive research; map product solutions to customer problems in a matrix (buyer problem, our solution, competitor solution); develop messaging for each problem with proof points; identify sales channels (direct, self-service, partner); measure results (units sold, engagement and objections, sales cycle length); help customers adopt and renew.
- **Tripp's ten steps (B2B SaaS):** understand the audience (including TAM, SAM, SOM); craft the value proposition with a message house; develop and validate the product with early adopters; pricing and packaging; build the sales team; marketing and lead generation; sales enablement (first-call deck, sales plays, objection handling, personas); customer success and retention; scalability; continuous iteration with customer advisory boards.
- **Wrike's five practices:** keep the launch journey simple and fit the GTM strategy on one page, with one message, channel and target market; create GTM and product together; do a full market review before building; test buyer personas for fit; go niche, then reuse the model to expand.
- **Predictive Index's six steps:** gain stakeholder alignment early (target market, number of customers, timeline, tactics, pricing); then three steps on matching executives' behavioral profiles to the plan (left out: it promotes PI's assessment); communicate the strategy internally; create a project plan with a project manager; hold each other accountable.
- **Pragmatic's order:** problem, buyer, value, then prioritize and differentiate; validate with interviews, win/loss analysis and feedback; choose where to focus; then positioning and messaging; then define success.

### 2.5 GTM motions and growth models

**PMA's four frameworks:**

- **Funnel:** awareness, consideration, decision; marketing owns the top and sales the bottom; leads move from marketing-qualified lead to sales-qualified lead to opportunity to close. PMA says most enterprise SaaS, Salesforce included, still runs its core pipeline this way.
- **Flywheel:** HubSpot introduced it in 2018 to replace its own funnel; customers sit at the center and marketing, sales and service work together to turn happy customers into referrals.
- **Product-led growth (PLG):** "The product does the selling." Users try it free, get value fast, then upgrade or bring in colleagues who become paying accounts. Example: Slack spreading team by team into paid enterprise deals.
- **Account-based marketing (ABM):** treat a small list of high-value accounts as individual markets with research and messaging built for each one. Example: GumGum's custom comic book for T-Mobile's CEO.
- PMA: "Most GTM strategies end up as a hybrid": PLG for self-serve customers plus ABM for accounts big enough to justify sales.

**Motions as listed by others:**

| Source | Motions |
| --- | --- |
| Product School | Sales-led (outbound, demos, multi-stakeholder deals); product-led (self-serve signup, in-product upgrades, usage-driven expansion); hybrid (self-serve for small customers, sales-assisted for large); partner-led or ecosystem-led (marketplaces, integrations, resellers) |
| Salesforce | Direct sales (long cycles, complex high-price products, high-touch); self-service (simple, low-price products, no rep); partner or channel sales (reach without headcount; marketplaces, resellers, distributors) |
| Dovetail | Direct sales, self-serve, channel partnerships, marketplace distribution; PLG and long-cycle enterprise sales as two models |
| Airtable "7 GTM motions" | Inbound, outbound, product-led, sales-led, marketing-led, ecosystem-led, event-led |
| AEVO distribution options | Direct sales, e-commerce, marketplaces, VAR or partner networks, social commerce, inside sales, product-led (freemium or free trial) |
| Kaizen | A dedicated sales team pays off only when the average purchase value is high (usually B2B); for low values a channel partner, store or e-commerce makes the sale |

Rules of thumb in the sources:

- Match the motion to the product, price point and buying process. A self-serve purchase doesn't fit a product that needs organizational change to adopt; enterprise sales doesn't fit a high-volume, low-cost product. A mismatched motion is a main cause of GTM failure [Dovetail].
- Align pricing tiers, bundling and motion with how the target segment buys [Wrike, PMA, Dovetail].
- If the model depends on a high-converting free trial, onboarding, in-product education and upgrade prompts are central to GTM, not extras [Product School].

### 2.6 Channels

- Concentrate on the few channels where the ICP actually looks for solutions instead of spreading thin [Aventi, Wrike, Product School: "choose deliberately, not to be everywhere"].
- Test channels and move budget from ones that don't gain traction after a fair test [PMA].
- Every channel needs its own hypothesis, measure and revenue-contribution estimate [AEVO].
- For each key channel, document what good looks like, who owns it and how success is measured [Product School].
- Sales channels are where people buy; distribution channels are how the product reaches them; they can be the same (direct) or a chain (wholesaler, retailer) [Coursera].
- Kaizen disagrees with focus: "the most effective way to go to market is on several channels simultaneously", and a multi-channel mix usually beats the most efficient single channel.
- Typical B2B channels: LinkedIn, webinars, sales-led demos, account-based outreach, industry events; B2C: paid social, influencers, retail and e-commerce, search [PMA].
- Channel partners: ask what's in it for them, since you may be one of thirty or forty vendors they carry; help drive their sales cycle [Aventi].
- Social media: use it to listen to influencers and customers, not only to advertise [Aventi].

### 2.7 Buying center [PMA, Aventi]

The people involved in a B2B purchase. PMA's roles: initiator (starts the process), user, influencer (convinces others), decision maker (final approval), buyer (owns the budget), approver (often C-suite, final final approval), gatekeeper (can block it). Titles overlap in small organizations. Map each role's objectives and pain points, then write messaging per role: an economic buyer cares about cost and ROI, an end user about day-to-day usability. Both PMA and Aventi cite Harvard Business Review: an average B2B purchase involves 6.8 people. General knowledge: this is from CEB research reported in HBR in 2017; I haven't verified the exact figure here.

Related: build the GTM around whoever makes the buying decision, not just who uses the product, especially in B2B2C or product-led SaaS with consumer-like signup [PMA].

### 2.8 Rollout waves [Product School]

1. **Internal alpha:** your own team or a small internal group catches obvious issues and checks basic value.
2. **Private beta:** a stable version for a small set of invited customers, for real-world feedback.
3. **Limited release for one segment:** a controlled launch to one target segment (an industry or region) to prove the GTM model before scaling.
4. **General availability:** officially live for all target customers through normal channels.
5. **Follow-up campaigns:** ongoing marketing and sales plays after launch for deeper adoption, upsell and new segments.

"Each wave has its own goals, its own feedback loops, and its own decision points about whether to expand, adjust, or pause." Dovetail similarly recommends validating through limited releases, pilots or targeted campaigns before a full launch. The wiki's "Launched" definition (first day the target segment can use it without an invitation) is built on these waves; Juan approved it on 2026-11-23.

### 2.9 Value proposition, positioning and messaging

**Positioning:**

- "The slot you claim in your customer's mental market map: the category you play in, the alternatives you are compared against, and the reason you are the better choice for this specific context" [Product School]. Only source in the batch that names category.
- "The unique space the product occupies in the customer's mind relative to other options" [Dovetail].
- Pick a position: the safest choice, the fastest way to value, the most flexible platform, the opinionated tool that does one thing best. "You do not need to be everything. You do need to be something specific for someone" [Product School].
- Hinge's options for a firm: innovator, thought leader, low-price option. Don't claim superior service with low-price positioning, and beware price wars and being unable to raise prices later.
- **Positioning comes before messaging** [Dovetail]: positioning is strategic, messaging is how it's said. Tripp: messaging and positioning are not the same thing.

**Value proposition:**

- Answers "why should I choose this product over its competitors?" [Kaizen]; names the customer, the problem and the differentiated solution [Dovetail]; should be as much about the target market as the product [Coursera]; covers what, who and why for this product even if the company has its own value proposition [Salesforce].
- Kaizen's three exercises: describe the competitive advantage (something customers value, hard to copy, profitable and sustainable); calculate the monetary value to the customer, benchmarked against competitors; write a unique selling proposition (USP), the single reason that sets the offering apart from both the competition and the best alternative.
- Why now, why this solution, why your company [AEVO]; "Differentiation is not a list of features, it is a promise of transformation supported by business outcomes" [AEVO].
- Pragmatic's three questions: what problem are we solving, who actually cares, and why us.

**Messaging:**

- Message per buying-center role [PMA]; per persona and use case, with core promise, proof points and key benefits [Product School]; a "messaging architecture that is simple enough to remember and flexible enough to adapt" [Product School].
- **Proof points:** Salesforce's method: for each buyer problem, write the product value, what competitors offer, and a key message with a proof point (example: shoes designed with an orthopedic surgeon; "60% of wearers felt less foot pain after a month"; invented example). Show measurable ROI.
- **Message house** framework for building the narrative [Tripp; no detail given].
- **StoryBrand / brand story A3:** Kaizen bases its message method on Donald Miller's *Building a StoryBrand*: find the customer's story and put the product at its core.
- **Customer as hero:** make the customer, not the product, the hero of the launch story; start from their challenges, aspirations and goals [Jeremy Castile of Docker, in PMA].
- Use customers' own language and test messaging before scaling [PMA]. Describe the ideal customer in objective, checkable terms ("ambitious" is not checkable) [Aventi].
- Don't lead with "AI-powered": buyers care about the problem solved, not the technology [Pragmatic].
- Message by buyer journey stage: educational or even funny content at awareness; demos and explainer videos near decision [Aventi, PMA].

**Positioning statement:** none of the sources gives a template. General knowledge: the common form is Moore's "For (target customer) who (need), (product) is a (category) that (benefit). Unlike (alternative), our product (differentiator)" from *Crossing the Chasm*. The wiki's overview template uses this shape.

### 2.10 Competitive analysis for GTM

- Two kinds of competition [Kaizen]: **known competitors** (where you lose customers once they decide to spend on the problem), analyzed for advantages (scale, marketing, retention) and tactics (pricing, packaging, features), plus service quality and why people like or dislike them; and **competitive alternatives**, what people would use if neither you nor direct competitors existed (for running wear: swimming, other cardio, generic clothing). First convince people they need the solution, then that yours is better.
- Look at obvious competitors, substitutes and non-consumption: what else might the customer "hire"? [Product School].
- Salesforce's guiding questions: what you offer that competitors don't; why a popular competitor resonates; which competitor tactics to adopt; which needs nobody serves.
- Win/loss analysis and customer advisory boards to refine, especially for enterprises with past launches [PMA, Pragmatic].
- Tools listed by AEVO: SWOT, Business Model Canvas, CRM. Info-Tech calls guessed competitive SWOT a cause of poor outcomes.
- Product-market fit analysis inside GTM: a market found, the product delivered to it, and customers satisfied; reached by testing an MVP with potential customers [Kaizen].

### 2.11 Pricing inside GTM

The wiki's pricing gap has its own research file; GTM sources add:

- Pricing and packaging signal who the product is for and where it sits; cover packaging and tiers, not just price points [Dovetail].
- Decide what you charge for (users, usage, seats, credits, features), tiers, free vs trial vs freemium vs paid, AI monetization, discounts and contract lengths [Product School]; estimate the break-even zone in advance [Product School].
- Kaizen: pricing dimensions (product, use, volume, customer or contract); differentiated pricing should match value, encourage upgrades, allow different prices by value and reward longer contracts; five factors: organizational goals, costs, customers, competition, value chain; keep prices easy to understand; anchor to something more expensive (half an employee's salary for a product that replaces their work); value selling vs discounting.
- Coursera's pricing questions: costs, profit, competitor prices, willingness to pay, subscription or transactional.
- Hinge: a low-price GTM can trap you when you need to raise prices.
- Info-Tech extends a GTM steering committee into a Product and Pricing Council to make product and pricing decisions structured instead of ad hoc.

### 2.12 Sales enablement

- Give customer-facing teams battlecards, demos, objection handling and clear handoffs before launch [Aventi, Wrike, Info-Tech, Salesforce].
- **Aventi's sales playbook contents:** scannable content; discovery questions; answers to "why buy, why now, why us?"; objection handling; competitive traps; up-to-date information; clear calls to action. Update it at least monthly. Keep sales content short, specific and differentiated, without hype.
- Tripp: first-call deck, sales plays, scripts, objection handling, personas.
- Pragmatic: sales enablement is the second thing to fix after clarity on problem and value; "just because something is a priority for product or marketing doesn't mean it's a priority for sales."

### 2.13 GTM team and governance

- **Team members:** product, product marketing, sales or sales enablement, customer success, pricing, maybe a GTM manager [PMA]; product development, sales, finance, marketing, customer service, plus fulfillment and partner teams, with sign-off from executives, legal and compliance [Salesforce]; marketing, sales and product management as the three core functions [Kaizen].
- **Start small:** a core group first, then add people at the right time (Aventi's Jeff Thompson and SAP's Helen Dwight, in PMA).
- **Steering committee, program manager and workstream leads** [Info-Tech]; strong program leadership, a full pass through all steps at least once, stakeholder buy-in, skills and discipline.
- **Governance:** meetings, information sharing and other standards for monitoring the GTM process [Kaizen].
- **Start with the goal and work backward:** define success, then ask each team what it needs [Pragmatic].
- **Short timelines:** treat a launch like an MVP: sort tasks into must-have, nice-to-have and post-launch (Castile of Docker, in PMA).

### 2.14 Success metrics for GTM

- **Write success as metric, amount and timeframe** (Steve Gaylor, in Pragmatic). Examples: "Increase trial-to-paid conversion by 15% within two quarters"; "Generate $3M in qualified pipeline within six months"; "Reach 25% adoption across existing enterprise accounts by year-end".
- **Outcomes, not activity:** an on-time launch is not a successful one; campaigns and enablement sessions show effort, not progress [Pragmatic, Product School citing Lisa Kamm: "did it have outcomes?"].
- **Product School's metric families:** acquisition (signups, demo requests, qualified pipeline, CAC); activation and adoption (time to value, activation rate, feature adoption); revenue (ARR added, ACV, win rate, trial or POC conversion); retention and expansion (logo retention, NRR, expansion rate). Pick "a small, coherent set" that fits the GTM's intent.
- **Three horizons** [Product School]: pre-launch validation (waitlist signups, discovery calls, early-access responses; depth over volume); launch and early adoption ("activation matters more than signups"); long-term efficiency (CAC and payback, deal size, NRR, LTV). Merge marketing and product analytics.
- **Sales-side measures:** CAC, cost per dollar of sales expense, close or conversion rate, sales cycle length [Coursera]; CAC, customer lifetime value, conversion rate [Salesforce]; units sold, engagement and objections, cycle length [Salesforce]; conversion rate, sales volume and time, lowering CAC and shortening the cycle [PMA].
- Set targets before launch, track them, and adjust if they aren't met [PMA, Wrike, Product School]. Decide what to look at in week one, month one and quarter one [Product School].
- Combine numbers with qualitative signals from sales calls, tickets, research and in-product feedback [Product School].

### 2.15 B2B vs B2C [PMA, Salesforce]

| | B2B | B2C |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Focus | Business problem for a buying committee | Personal need or want |
| Messaging | ROI, efficiency, risk reduction, integration | Emotion, lifestyle, convenience, price, identity |
| Sales cycle | Weeks to months, sometimes 12+ months for enterprise | Minutes to days |
| Channels | LinkedIn, webinars, demos, account-based outreach, events | Paid social, influencers, retail and e-commerce, search |

Salesforce: B2C is more transactional with one decision-maker; B2B is relationship-driven with many stakeholders, email, white papers and webinars.

### 2.16 Adoption and post-launch

- Many teams stop after launch; helping customers adopt and renew matters as much: training, a single point of contact, feedback loops, renewal management, upsell and cross-sell [Salesforce].
- Customer success and retention belong in GTM [Tripp].
- Scale-ups should keep refining from previous launches' data and improve customer experience (design, accessibility, cognitive load, pricing, convenience, availability) [PMA].
- A/B testing suits incremental changes with enough traffic, not deploying a new strategy or disrupting a market ("Everything can be A/B tested, but not everything should be", Siddhartha Kathpalia of VWO, in PMA).

### 2.17 AI in GTM

- Useful for summarizing interviews, generating messaging variants, drafting campaign assets and analyzing performance data; risky when it replaces thinking, since it doesn't know your product, market or customers [Pragmatic].
- Product School: use AI to explore positioning angles and test messages, mine calls and tickets for objections, cluster segments from usage and CRM data, personalize outbound and trigger in-app nudges; not to "invent" positioning.

## 3. Practices most sources agree on

1. **Start GTM during development, not at the end,** so market insight shapes the product and positioning [Pragmatic, Wrike, Salesforce, Info-Tech, Castile in PMA].
2. **Pick a narrow target first** and expand later: an ICP that is a high-fit subset of the market [PMA, Dovetail, Wrike, Hinge, Product School]. Oatly is the batch's main example.
3. **Ground positioning in research,** not internal assumptions: interviews, win/loss, existing customers [Pragmatic, Dovetail, Salesforce, Info-Tech].
4. **Differentiate against named alternatives,** including doing nothing and substitutes [Pragmatic, Kaizen, Product School, Salesforce].
5. **Align product, marketing and sales on one story;** Pragmatic calls alignment "a clarity problem", not a coordination problem [Pragmatic, Dovetail, Product School, AEVO].
6. **Name an owner;** shared responsibility without accountability drops work [Pragmatic, Product School].
7. **Enable sales before launch** [Aventi, Pragmatic, Salesforce, Tripp, Info-Tech].
8. **Set measurable success criteria before launch** and judge outcomes, not activity [Pragmatic, PMA, Product School].
9. **Validate before full commitment** with tests, pilots or limited releases [Dovetail, Product School, AEVO, PMA].
10. **Treat GTM as a loop:** feed what you learn back into ICP, messaging, pricing and channels [Product School, Salesforce, Hinge, Tripp].

**Common mistakes the sources list:** starting too late; unclear problem definition; no clear owner; weak sales enablement; not involving stakeholders early; tactics without strategy; treating GTM as a checklist of templates; optimizing for deadlines instead of outcomes; messaging based on internal assumptions [Pragmatic]. Wrong target customer (too small, unwilling to pay, hard to reach) as the most common root cause; undifferentiated positioning; mismatched sales motion; lack of alignment [Dovetail]. Shallow research, misaligned communication, launching without testing [AEVO]. Making the product the hero of the story [Castile in PMA]. Targeting every segment with one message, or giving every feature equal weight [Pragmatic].

## 4. Where sources conflict

1. **Who owns GTM.**
   - Product marketing: PMA ("more often than not, product marketers own the go-to-market process"; best when "reporting to marketing while being backed by executive teams"), Aventi ("as product marketing leaders, you own GTM strategy"), Dovetail ("most commonly led by product marketing"), Coursera (the PMM prepares it).
   - Revenue operations creates and maintains it; sales leaders assemble the GTM team: Salesforce.
   - Shared, with PM owning market understanding and strategic direction and PMM leading positioning, launch coordination and enablement: Pragmatic.
   - Depends on stage: the PM owns most of it when there's no PMM; once there is one, the PMM leads and is accountable for the plan; sales, CS and RevOps are co-authors: Product School. Its split: product owns what, why and who it's for; product marketing owns how the story is told; sales and CS own turning it into revenue and retention.
   - Kaizen: marketing defines target, value proposition, messages and channels; product management defines the product, USP, vision, roadmap, and also target markets, pricing and distribution.
   - Conflict inside Kaizen: it gives both marketing and product management the job of defining the target market.
2. **One plan per launch vs a lasting system.** Time-bound and launch-specific: PMA, Dovetail ("written for specific moments… rather than as permanent documents"), Coursera, Airtable, Wrike ("not a long-term business plan but a short-term actionable roadmap"). Lasting: AEVO ("an operating system"), Info-Tech (foundational decisions such as tech stack, channel strategy and pricing structure are made once; later releases are "check-listed against prior foundational decisions"), Salesforce (keep updating it), Product School ("a repeatable cycle instead of a one-time launch artefact").
3. **Whether market sizing belongs in GTM.** In: Info-Tech (size the obtainable market in phase 1), Kaizen (total available market, segmented market, market share), Tripp (TAM, SAM, SOM), PMA (market size shapes the plan). Out: Product School ("You are not defining your total addressable market"). The wiki keeps market size on the strategy page.
4. **Focus vs many channels.** Few channels: Aventi, Wrike (one channel), Product School, PMA. Many at once: Kaizen.
5. **How often to revisit.** At least quarterly and with the annual budget, personas quarterly (Salesforce); playbook monthly (Aventi); "adjust your GTM in weeks, not quarters" (Product School); "nothing should be carved in stone" (Hinge).
6. **Motion taxonomy.** Airtable's seven motions mix motions (product-led, sales-led) with channels (inbound, outbound, events). PMA mixes growth models (funnel, flywheel) with motions (PLG) and tactics (ABM). Product School, Salesforce and Dovetail keep motion separate from channels.
7. **What success means.** Acquisition and sales efficiency (CAC, cycle length, close rate, pipeline: Coursera, Salesforce, PMA) vs activation and value first (Product School: "activation matters more than signups"; Pragmatic: outcomes over activity).
8. **Who the GTM is for.** Mostly "launching a new product"; Info-Tech and PMA stretch it to acquisitions, rebrands and changing business focus; Hinge applies it to professional services firms.
9. **Templates.** Pragmatic warns against "treating GTM like a checklist" of templates; Airtable, PMA (template, tier framework, launch checklist) and Product School (launch checklist) offer them, and Info-Tech says later releases should be check-listed.
10. **The 4 Ps as GTM.** Coursera presents the 4 Ps as the GTM framework; no other source does.

## 6. Vocabulary

- **GTM / go-to-market strategy:** see section 1; sources disagree on whether it's per launch or lasting.
- **GTM plan:** used interchangeably with GTM strategy by most sources; Pragmatic and Product School separate the strategy (choices) from execution (tasks, calendar).
- **GTM motion:** how the product is sold and reaches customers: product-led, sales-led, partner-led, hybrid (Product School). Some sources (Airtable) include channels.
- **Routes to market:** Info-Tech's term for channels and distribution, chosen with the motion.
- **ICP (ideal customer profile):** "a high-fit subset of your broader market" (AI summary, also PMA); company-level profile (industry, size, region, tech stack) vs personas at the individual level (Product School); describe it in objective, checkable terms (Aventi). Product School's "living segmentation" updates it from usage and CRM data. AEVO frames ICPs as "total addressable problem segments": who feels the pain most, has budget and urgency, and benefits most.
- **Buyer persona:** a profile of a buyer or user role, built from customer data and interviews (Salesforce, Coursera); review quarterly (Salesforce).
- **Buying center / buying group:** everyone involved in a B2B purchase (PMA, Info-Tech).
- **Value proposition:** the benefits that make someone choose you over alternatives (Kaizen, Coursera, Dovetail).
- **USP (unique selling proposition):** the single reason the offering beats both competitors and the best alternative (Kaizen).
- **Positioning:** the place the product holds in the customer's mind against alternatives (Dovetail, Product School); strategic, and comes before messaging.
- **Messaging:** positioning expressed in words, per audience and channel (Dovetail, Product School).
- **Message house / messaging architecture:** a structure for core promise, pillars and proof points (Tripp, Product School).
- **Proof point:** evidence behind a claim (Salesforce, Product School).
- **Competitive alternatives:** what customers would use if no direct competitor existed (Kaizen).
- **Sales enablement:** materials and training that let sales and CS sell and support the product (Aventi, Salesforce).
- **Sales playbook / battlecard:** sales enablement documents (Aventi).
- **Launch readiness:** marketing, sales and CS ready when the product ships; executive readiness sign-off (Info-Tech).
- **Launch tier:** a launch's size class; Product School says the PMM defines launch tiers, and PMA sells a "product launch tier framework" template. No source in the batch defines the tiers.
- **Rollout waves:** alpha, private beta, limited release, GA, follow-up campaigns (Product School).
- **GA (general availability):** live for all target customers (Product School).
- **MQL, SQL:** marketing-qualified and sales-qualified lead, stages in the funnel (PMA).
- **PLG, ABM:** product-led growth; account-based marketing (PMA).
- **CAC, LTV, NRR, ACV, ARR:** customer acquisition cost, lifetime value, net revenue retention, annual contract value, annual recurring revenue (Product School, Salesforce, Coursera).
- **CAB:** customer advisory board (PMA, Tripp).
- **PPC (Product and Pricing Council):** Info-Tech's suggested standing body for product and pricing decisions.
- **Revenue lifecycle management:** Salesforce's term for the RevOps-run process that maintains GTM.
